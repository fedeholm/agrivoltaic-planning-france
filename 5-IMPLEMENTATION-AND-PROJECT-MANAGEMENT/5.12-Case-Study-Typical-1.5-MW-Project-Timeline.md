# Section 5.12: Case Study — Typical 1.5 MW Project Timeline

## Introduction

This illustrative case study shows how development, permitting, financing, construction, and agricultural workstreams can be sequenced for a typical 1.5 MW agrivoltaic project in France. It assumes a commercially sized project with a defined site and a grid connection to be secured. Actual durations and responsibilities will vary with site conditions, agricultural calendars, planning and environmental requirements, grid capacity, procurement lead times, and financing decisions.

The phases overlap deliberately: feasibility, design, permitting, and financing activities should progress in parallel where their inputs allow. The schedule from site selection to operation is approximately **36 months in the planned case**, with a **36–48 month overall estimate** to allow for project-specific delays and approval or grid-connection lead times.

## 5.12.1 Phase-by-Phase Timeline

| Project period | Phase and principal activities | Indicative milestone |
|---|---|---|
| **Months 0–3** | **Pre-project and site selection:** identify candidate sites; screen solar resource and agricultural feasibility; begin engagement with landowners, farmers, local stakeholders, and authorities; prepare a preliminary business case. | Preferred site selected and initial go/no-go decision documented. |
| **Months 3–9** | **Detailed feasibility and early financing:** complete solar, agricultural, environmental, and site assessments; analyze eligibility for relevant subsidy programmes; consult planning authorities before application; commission environmental studies as needed; hold preliminary discussions with banks. | Feasibility findings, project concept, and financing assumptions are sufficiently developed to proceed. |
| **Months 6–15** | **Design and permitting:** advance engineering from concept to detailed design; submit the planning application and relevant subsidy applications; run competitive contractor procurement; complete required environmental review and respond to authority requests. | Required approvals and an investable, coordinated design are progressing toward a construction-ready project. |
| **Months 12–18** | **Financing approval and contract signature:** secure conditional subsidy decisions where applicable, obtain bank approval, finalize the grid-connection agreement, award construction contracts, and arrange insurance. | Financial close and construction-readiness conditions are met before full mobilization. |
| **Months 15–33** | **Construction:** months 15–18, foundations and site infrastructure; months 15–28, equipment procurement and delivery; months 21–30, PV panel installation and electrical works; months 18–33, agricultural systems; months 30–33, commissioning preparation. | Mechanical completion and construction handover packages are ready for testing. |
| **Months 33–36** | **Commissioning and handover:** complete electrical and safety testing; obtain grid synchronization and connection; verify performance; train operators; activate the applicable electricity-sale or support arrangements and hand over the facility. | Safe, documented transition to commercial operation. |
| **Month 36 onward** | **Operations:** operate both energy and agricultural systems; optimize performance; carry out routine maintenance and annual reporting. | Stable operation with energy and agricultural outcomes monitored against their respective baselines and plans. |

The month ranges are planning assumptions, not guaranteed approval or construction durations. A delayed grid connection, additional environmental surveys, seasonal construction constraints, appeals, or long-lead equipment can extend the schedule. Maintain an integrated schedule with explicit approval, grid, agricultural-calendar, and financial-close dependencies. The project duration estimate remains **36–48 months from site selection to operation**.

## 5.13 Resource Requirements by Phase

The following staffing levels are indicative project-team allocations. FTE means full-time equivalent; specialist workloads may be intermittent, and operating responsibilities should be confirmed before handover.

### Key personnel

| Role | Indicative allocation | Main responsibilities |
|---|---|---|
| Project Manager | **1.0 FTE, months 0–36** | Coordinate schedule, budget, decisions, stakeholders, interfaces, and readiness for handover. |
| Agricultural Manager or Advisor | **0.5 FTE, months 6–36; full-time from month 36** | Translate farm requirements into design and construction decisions; establish and manage the operating agricultural plan. |
| Technical Engineer | **0.5 FTE, months 6–18; on call thereafter** | Coordinate technical design, grid and contractor interfaces, technical reviews, and commissioning support. |
| Financial Manager | **0.25 FTE, months 0–36; ongoing from month 36** | Maintain the business case and cash-flow model; coordinate subsidy and lender information; track financial performance. |
| Safety and Compliance Officer | **0.25 FTE, months 15–36** | Coordinate construction safety, compliance records, inspections, and readiness for safe operation. |
| Environmental Coordinator | **0.25 FTE, months 3–24** | Coordinate surveys, mitigation commitments, permitting inputs, and environmental measures during development and construction preparation. |

### External consultants and contractors

- **Feasibility consultant (months 0–6):** coordinate or deliver site screening and the initial integrated feasibility work.
- **Environmental specialist (months 3–15):** conduct required baseline surveys and environmental assessment, and support the permitting process.
- **Grid-connection engineer (months 6–18):** support the connection application, technical studies, and agreement review.
- **EPC contractor (months 15–33):** deliver the contracted engineering, procurement, and construction scope.
- **Electrical inspector (months 30–36):** perform required independent inspections and testing.
- **Commissioning engineer (months 33–36):** coordinate system testing, performance verification, and technical handover.

Other project-specific specialists may be needed for planning, legal, geotechnical, ecological, insurance, agricultural, or tax advice. Confirm consultant scopes and independence requirements against the actual permitting and financing plan.

### Management and professional-services budget

Allow approximately **€150,000–€300,000**, or around **8–17% of project CapEx**, as an initial planning range for management and professional services. This is an indicative allowance, not a quotation: refine it against the project scope, site complexity, required surveys and studies, permitting pathway, procurement model, and financing requirements. Keep it distinct from equipment, construction, grid-connection, and other direct CapEx budgets to avoid double counting.

## 5.14 Success Metrics and Key Performance Indicators

Set baselines, owners, data sources, reporting frequency, and acceptance criteria for each KPI before work begins. Record approved changes and explain material deviations rather than changing the baseline without traceability.

### Construction phase

- **Schedule adherence:** actual milestone dates compared with the approved baseline; report forecast completion and critical-path slippage.
- **Budget control:** forecast final cost and actual cost compared with the approved budget; explain variance and approved changes.
- **Safety performance:** record incidents and near misses, with a **zero-incident target** and prompt investigation of every event.
- **Quality:** track inspections, non-conformities, and punch-list items; report the share closed by the agreed handover date and any safety-critical open items.
- **Stakeholder satisfaction:** gather and review feedback from the farmer, landowner, local stakeholders, and project partners at agreed milestones.

### Operational phase

- **Energy production:** compare metered production with the forecast, normalized or explained for irradiation, availability, and curtailment where data allows.
- **Agricultural yield:** compare production and relevant quality indicators with the documented agricultural baseline, accounting for crop, season, and management changes.
- **System uptime:** track technical availability against the **>99% target**, with exclusions and calculation method defined in advance.
- **Financial performance:** compare operating costs, revenues, and cash flow with the approved budget and financial model.
- **Return on investment:** update the long-term forecast and monitor progress against the project’s approved IRR or other investment hurdle.
- **Employee and operator satisfaction:** review feedback and recurring operational issues at least annually.
- **Environmental and biodiversity outcomes:** monitor the site-specific indicators and commitments established in the environmental and management plans.

## 5.15 Decommissioning and End-of-Life Planning

Plan for end-of-life during development, then review the plan at design changes, major equipment replacements, and periodic operations reviews. Confirm applicable legal, planning, contractual, land-lease, producer, and financing obligations with qualified advisers; the requirements and available recycling routes can change over a project’s lifetime.

### 5.15.1 Lifecycle planning (months 12–36 and ongoing)

- Select equipment with documented recyclability information and identify available manufacturer take-back or recycling arrangements.
- Record expected replacement and disposal pathways for inverters, which may require replacement on an indicative **10–15 year** cycle.
- Define land-restoration obligations, if applicable, and establish a practical method for verifying restoration.
- Plan the expected life, maintenance, replacement, and eventual phase-out of agricultural infrastructure alongside the PV system.
- Maintain an asset register and update lifecycle cost and decommissioning assumptions during operations.

### 5.15.2 Decommissioning strategy (year 25 onward)

- Plan for safe removal, recycling, and material recovery of PV modules and associated equipment, using qualified contractors and approved waste routes.
- Estimate salvage value and the costs of dismantling, transport, recycling, and site restoration; refresh estimates as market conditions and equipment change.
- Carry out environmental restoration where required by permits, contracts, or site conditions, and document the completed work.
- Agree with the landowner and relevant authorities how the site will be used after decommissioning.
- Establish and periodically review a financial reserve. **€50,000–€150,000** is an initial planning range only; replace it with a project-specific estimate that reflects the equipment, site, contractual obligations, and future cost assumptions.

## Conclusion

A 1.5 MW project can reach operation in about three years when parallel workstreams and approval dependencies are managed effectively, but a 36–48 month planning window is more resilient. Clear staffing, measurable construction and operating KPIs, and lifecycle provisions help ensure that project delivery remains aligned with agricultural production and end-of-life responsibilities.
