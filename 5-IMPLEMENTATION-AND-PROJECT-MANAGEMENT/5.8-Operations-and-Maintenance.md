# Section 5.8: Operations and Maintenance

## Introduction

The Operations and Maintenance (O&M) phase begins after commissioning and continues throughout the project's operating life, typically 20–25 years or longer. The objective is to keep the solar, agricultural, environmental, and monitoring systems safe, productive, compliant, and financially reliable.

Agrivoltaic O&M is more complex than conventional PV maintenance because two production systems share the same land and infrastructure. Maintenance activities must therefore be coordinated with crop cycles, livestock movements, irrigation, harvesting, biodiversity management, weather, and grid requirements.

A successful O&M programme should:

- Maintain safe and reliable energy production
- Preserve agricultural productivity and access
- Protect soil, water, habitats, and biodiversity
- Detect and correct faults before they cause major losses
- Control operating costs and plan replacement expenditure
- Maintain warranties, insurance, permits, and compliance records
- Provide accurate performance data to owners, lenders, regulators, and operators
- Support continuous improvement throughout the project life

> **Important:** Electrical, structural, agricultural, and environmental maintenance should be performed by competent personnel under approved procedures. Live electrical work must be carried out only by suitably qualified and authorized workers.

## 5.8.1 O&M Organization and Responsibilities

### Core responsibilities

| Role | Main responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Asset owner** | Financial performance, major decisions, contracts, insurance, compliance |
| **Site operations manager** | Daily coordination, safety, access, reporting, contractor management |
| **Farmer/agricultural operator** | Crops, livestock, irrigation, harvesting, agricultural records |
| **PV O&M provider** | Monitoring, preventive maintenance, corrective repairs, warranties |
| **Electrical specialist** | Testing, protection systems, grid compliance, major fault response |
| **Environmental advisor** | Biodiversity, soil, water, habitat, and mitigation monitoring |
| **Monitoring provider** | Data platform, communications, alarms, backups, cybersecurity |
| **Insurance provider** | Risk review, claims, loss prevention, renewal requirements |

### Responsibility matrix

Define responsibility for each activity using a RACI matrix:

- **Responsible:** Performs the activity
- **Accountable:** Owns the result
- **Consulted:** Provides technical or operational input
- **Informed:** Receives the result or report

Example:

| Activity | Owner | Site manager | Farmer | PV O&M | Environmental advisor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Daily PV monitoring | A | R | I | R | I |
| Crop management | I | C | A/R | I | C |
| Irrigation maintenance | I | C | A/R | I | C |
| Inverter service | A | C | I | R | I |
| Biodiversity survey | A | C | C | I | R |
| Emergency response | A | R | C | R | C |
| Annual performance review | A/R | R | C | C | C |

The matrix should be adapted to the contractual structure and updated when personnel or service providers change.

### Staffing models

#### Owner-operated model

The owner employs or directly manages the operations team.

**Benefits:** Maximum control and direct knowledge of the site.  
**Challenges:** Requires internal expertise, coverage for absences, and management of specialist contractors.

#### Outsourced O&M model

A specialist provider manages PV monitoring and maintenance under a service contract.

**Benefits:** Access to technical expertise, 24-hour monitoring, and established spare-parts networks.  
**Challenges:** Requires careful contract management and clear boundaries with the farmer.

#### Hybrid model

PV maintenance is outsourced while agricultural operations remain with the farmer or agricultural cooperative.

This is often appropriate for agrivoltaic projects because agricultural decisions should remain closely connected to the operator who understands the land, crops, livestock, and market.

## 5.8.2 Daily, Weekly, and Monthly Operations

### Daily operations

Daily activities should be proportionate to project size and automation, but normally include:

- Reviewing the monitoring dashboard
- Checking system availability and active alarms
- Comparing production with expected conditions
- Checking inverter, transformer, and communications status
- Reviewing weather and grid-curtailment conditions
- Inspecting livestock, fencing, gates, or crop areas where relevant
- Checking irrigation status during the growing season
- Recording unusual conditions, incidents, or access restrictions

A daily inspection does not always require a physical site visit if remote monitoring is reliable. However, physical inspections should occur at a frequency appropriate to risk and should increase after storms, faults, vandalism, or major agricultural activity.

### Weekly operations

Weekly activities may include:

- Reviewing production trends and fault history
- Walking representative areas of the array
- Inspecting modules, cables, structures, fences, and access roads
- Checking vegetation height and shading around equipment
- Checking drainage, erosion, ponding, and sediment movement
- Reviewing irrigation pressure, flow, and soil-moisture data
- Coordinating upcoming farm and maintenance activities
- Reviewing open work orders and overdue corrective actions

### Monthly operations

Monthly activities should include:

- Formal energy-performance review
- Availability and downtime analysis
- Inspection of inverters, transformers, switchgear, and monitoring equipment
- Review of agricultural production and input records
- Review of soil moisture, water use, and irrigation performance
- Inspection of roads, gates, fences, storage, and safety signs
- Review of environmental observations and complaints
- Update of the risk, issue, and maintenance registers
- Review of budget, invoices, and forecast operating costs
- Monthly report to the owner or asset manager

## 5.8.3 Preventive Maintenance Programme

Preventive maintenance reduces unplanned downtime and extends equipment life. The programme should combine manufacturer instructions, risk assessment, operating history, warranty conditions, and regulatory requirements.

### PV modules and mounting structures

Typical activities include:

- Visual inspection for cracks, delamination, discoloration, or hot spots
- Inspection of connectors, cable clips, and cable support
- Thermographic inspection where appropriate
- Assessment of soiling and cleaning need
- Inspection of mounting bolts, clamps, bracing, and corrosion
- Checking panel alignment and structural movement
- Inspection for shading from vegetation or new obstructions
- Inspection after severe wind, hail, snow, or impact events

### Inverters and electrical equipment

Typical activities include:

- Inspection of ventilation and cooling systems
- Cleaning filters, fans, and air paths
- Checking alarms and event logs
- Inspecting terminals and cable glands
- Thermal scanning of electrical connections
- Testing isolators, circuit breakers, and protective devices
- Reviewing firmware and settings under change control
- Checking enclosure seals and signs of moisture
- Inspecting transformers, switchgear, and medium-voltage equipment

Electrical testing intervals should be defined by the responsible engineer, manufacturer, insurer, and applicable requirements.

### Civil infrastructure

Inspect and maintain:

- Access roads and turning areas
- Drainage channels, culverts, swales, and outlets
- Foundations and visible structural components
- Fences, gates, locks, and signs
- Equipment pads and hardstanding
- Storage facilities and shelters
- Cable routes and warning markers
- Erosion-control measures

### Irrigation and agricultural infrastructure

Maintain:

- Pumps, filters, valves, and pressure regulators
- Drip lines, sprinklers, and distribution pipes
- Water tanks and level controls
- Flow meters and irrigation controllers
- Fences, gates, troughs, shelters, and handling areas
- Farm machinery access routes
- Crop supports, trellises, or orchard infrastructure

### Environmental measures

Maintain:

- Hedgerows and planted buffers
- Wildflower strips and meadow areas
- Ponds, swales, and wetland features
- Nest boxes and wildlife structures
- Erosion-control vegetation
- Habitat corridors
- Lighting restrictions and ecological exclusion zones

Maintenance should avoid undermining the biodiversity objectives that justified the measures.

## 5.8.4 Corrective Maintenance and Fault Response

### Fault-response process

Use a consistent process for all faults:

1. **Detection:** Alarm, inspection, operator report, or performance anomaly
2. **Classification:** Safety-critical, production-critical, agricultural, environmental, or routine
3. **Remote diagnosis:** Review monitoring data, event logs, weather, and recent work
4. **Dispatch:** Assign competent personnel and required equipment
5. **Isolation:** Apply lockout/tagout and safe-access procedures
6. **Repair:** Correct the defect or implement a safe temporary solution
7. **Testing:** Confirm safe and normal operation
8. **Close-out:** Record cause, action, downtime, cost, and lessons learned

### Fault priority levels

| Priority | Example | Target response |
|---|---|---|
| **P1 – Emergency** | Fire, exposed live conductors, serious injury, major environmental release | Immediate escalation and safe isolation |
| **P2 – Critical** | Major inverter failure, transformer trip, grid protection fault | Same day or within agreed service level |
| **P3 – Significant** | Underperforming strings, irrigation failure, damaged fencing | Planned response within 24–72 hours |
| **P4 – Routine** | Minor corrosion, signage, cosmetic damage, low-impact sensor fault | Include in planned maintenance |

Response targets should be defined in the O&M contract and adjusted for remoteness, weather, safety, and equipment availability.

### Root-cause analysis

Repeated or high-impact failures require more than component replacement. Investigate:

- Design or installation defects
- Incorrect settings or firmware
- Environmental exposure
- Poor maintenance practice
- Inadequate training
- Supplier or component quality
- Grid disturbances
- Agricultural or vehicle impact
- Water ingress or drainage failure

Document corrective actions that prevent recurrence.

## 5.8.5 Agricultural Operations During the Operating Life

### Crop management

The agricultural plan should define:

- Crop rotation or perennial management
- Planting and harvest windows
- Shade and microclimate monitoring
- Fertilizer and crop-protection practices
- Irrigation strategy
- Machinery routes and working widths
- Areas reserved for maintenance access
- Product quality and market requirements
- Yield and revenue targets

Crop decisions should be reviewed using actual performance data from the site, not only initial assumptions.

### Livestock management

Where livestock are integrated, manage:

- Stocking rates
- Water supply
- Shelter and shade
- Fencing and gates
- Animal health and welfare
- Safe separation from electrical equipment
- Access for veterinary and emergency services
- Interaction between grazing and vegetation management

Electrical equipment should be protected from animal contact, rubbing, chewing, and manure accumulation.

### Machinery and access coordination

Establish a shared calendar for:

- Planting
- Spraying or crop protection
- Harvesting
- Grazing or animal movement
- PV maintenance
- Module cleaning
- Vegetation cutting
- Electrical inspections
- Deliveries and repairs

Access conflicts are a common source of operational disruption. The calendar should identify restricted zones, contact persons, and notice periods.

## 5.8.6 Soil, Water, and Biodiversity Management

### Soil monitoring

Monitor at representative locations inside, between, and outside the PV area. Parameters may include:

- Soil organic matter
- pH and nutrient status
- Bulk density and compaction
- Infiltration rate
- Moisture content
- Erosion indicators
- Biological activity or earthworm abundance
- Contamination indicators where relevant

Compare results against the pre-construction baseline and control areas.

### Water management

Track:

- Irrigation volume and timing
- Rainfall and soil moisture
- Water-table or pond levels where relevant
- Drainage performance
- Sediment movement
- Water quality
- Concentrated runoff from panel edges

Investigate persistent ponding, erosion, declining infiltration, or unexplained changes in irrigation demand.

### Biodiversity management

A practical monitoring programme may include:

- Vegetation surveys
- Pollinator or invertebrate observations
- Bird point counts
- Bat acoustic monitoring
- Amphibian or reptile surveys where relevant
- Hedgerow and habitat-condition scoring
- Invasive-species checks

The frequency should reflect permit conditions, ecological sensitivity, and the project's biodiversity objectives. Results should be used to adapt mowing, grazing, planting, lighting, drainage, and access practices.

## 5.8.7 Performance Monitoring and KPIs

### Energy KPIs

- Monthly and annual energy production
- Specific yield per installed kW
- Performance ratio
- System availability
- Inverter availability
- Curtailment hours and energy
- Downtime by cause
- Soiling losses
- Degradation trend
- Forecast versus actual production

### Agricultural KPIs

- Yield per hectare
- Product quality
- Agricultural revenue
- Water use per unit of output
- Input cost per hectare
- Labour hours
- Crop establishment success
- Livestock stocking rate and welfare measures
- Percentage of planned agricultural area actively used

### Environmental KPIs

- Soil organic matter and compaction
- Erosion incidents
- Irrigation volume and water efficiency
- Habitat area maintained
- Species or biodiversity indicators
- Hedgerow survival
- Water-quality indicators
- Environmental incidents and corrective actions

### Financial and operational KPIs

- Operating cost per kW or MWh
- Maintenance cost by equipment category
- Spare-parts cost
- Revenue versus budget
- Insurance claims
- Work-order closure time
- Preventive maintenance completion rate
- Safety incidents and near misses
- Community complaints and resolution time

A KPI should have a defined data source, calculation method, reporting frequency, target, owner, and escalation threshold.

## 5.8.8 Monitoring, Reporting, and Data Management

### Data architecture

The O&M system should define:

- Sensor and meter inventory
- Data owners and access rights
- Time synchronization
- Data retention period
- Backup frequency
- Cybersecurity controls
- Alarm thresholds
- Integration with financial and agricultural records
- Export formats for reports and audits

### Routine reports

#### Daily or automated report

- Production and availability
- Active alarms
- Grid status and curtailment
- Weather conditions
- Critical agricultural or irrigation alerts

#### Monthly report

- Energy production versus forecast
- Downtime and fault analysis
- Maintenance completed and overdue
- Agricultural activity and production status
- Water use and irrigation performance
- Environmental observations
- Safety and incident summary
- Cost and invoice status
- Risks and required decisions

#### Annual report

- Full energy-performance review
- Agricultural yield and financial review
- Soil, water, and biodiversity results
- Equipment degradation and replacement forecast
- Insurance and compliance status
- Budget variance and next-year plan
- Stakeholder engagement summary
- Updated risk register

### Data quality controls

- Check for missing or duplicate data
- Validate sensor values against expected ranges
- Compare meters and sub-meter totals
- Flag changes in sensor calibration
- Keep an audit trail for manual corrections
- Back up critical operational records
- Restrict changes to performance data and financial records

## 5.8.9 Maintenance Planning and Spare Parts

### Maintenance planning cycle

Use the following planning cycle:

1. Review manufacturer recommendations and warranty requirements.
2. Review operating history and failure data.
3. Identify critical equipment and single points of failure.
4. Plan preventive tasks by frequency and season.
5. Schedule maintenance around agricultural operations and grid restrictions.
6. Allocate staff, contractors, equipment, and spares.
7. Issue work orders and safety documentation.
8. Verify completion and record findings.
9. Update future maintenance intervals based on evidence.

### Critical spare parts

Depending on technology and risk, consider holding:

- Fuses and surge-protection devices
- Connectors and PV cable
- Inverter fans, filters, and control boards
- Communications equipment
- Sensors and data loggers
- Switchgear components
- Relays and protection devices
- Irrigation valves, filters, and control parts
- Pump components
- Structural fasteners and clamps
- Fencing, gates, and safety signs

High-value spares should be protected against moisture, temperature, theft, and unauthorized use.

### Replacement planning

Some components have shorter expected lives than the PV modules:

- Inverter components may require repair or replacement during the project life.
- Batteries, if present, require a separate replacement and safety plan.
- Irrigation controls, pumps, and sensors may require periodic replacement.
- Communications and monitoring equipment may become obsolete.
- Vegetation, fencing, and access infrastructure require ongoing renewal.

Create a long-term replacement reserve and update it using actual failure and price data.

## 5.8.10 Safety, Emergency Response, and Security

### Operational safety

Maintain procedures for:

- Electrical isolation and lockout/tagout
- Working at height
- Arc-flash and energized equipment
- Lifting and manual handling
- Hot work
- Confined spaces
- Vehicle and machinery movement
- Livestock interaction
- Chemical and fuel handling
- Severe weather
- Lone working

### Emergency scenarios

Prepare and rehearse response plans for:

- Electrical fire or equipment fire
- Electric shock or serious injury
- Storm or lightning damage
- Flooding or erosion
- Transformer oil or chemical spill
- Major inverter or grid fault
- Livestock escape or injury
- Security breach or vandalism
- Cybersecurity incident
- Extreme heat or wildfire risk

Emergency plans should include site maps, isolation points, access routes, emergency contacts, assembly areas, and instructions for responders.

### Security

Security measures may include:

- Perimeter fencing and controlled gates
- Intrusion alarms
- CCTV or remote cameras
- Lighting designed to limit ecological disturbance
- Asset marking and serial-number records
- Visitor sign-in and escort procedures
- Cybersecurity controls for monitoring and remote access

Security should be proportionate to risk and should not create unnecessary barriers to farming or wildlife movement.

## 5.8.11 Warranty, Insurance, and Compliance Management

### Warranty management

Maintain a warranty register containing:

- Equipment and serial number
- Supplier and warranty provider
- Warranty start and end dates
- Coverage and exclusions
- Required maintenance conditions
- Claim procedure and contact details
- Open claims and resolution status

Document faults thoroughly before making a warranty claim. Preserve failed components where the supplier requires inspection.

### Insurance management

Review annually:

- Property and equipment cover
- Business-interruption cover
- Public and employer liability
- Environmental liability
- Agricultural and livestock cover
- Cybersecurity cover
- Construction or maintenance contractor cover
- Natural-peril exclusions and deductibles

Notify insurers of material modifications, major incidents, changes in land use, or changes in operating responsibility.

### Regulatory and contractual compliance

Track:

- Grid-connection obligations
- Environmental permit conditions
- Agricultural-use commitments
- Subsidy reporting requirements
- Safety inspections and certifications
- Tax and financial reporting
- Land-lease obligations
- Insurance requirements
- Decommissioning and restoration obligations

A compliance calendar should identify deadlines, responsible persons, required evidence, and escalation arrangements.

---

## 5.8.12 Annual Operations Review

At least once each year, hold a formal review involving the owner, site manager, farmer, PV O&M provider, environmental advisor, and relevant technical specialists.

Review:

1. Energy performance and degradation
2. Agricultural production and revenue
3. Environmental and biodiversity outcomes
4. Soil and water condition
5. Safety and incidents
6. Maintenance completion and overdue work
7. Equipment failures and recurring faults
8. Operating costs and budget variance
9. Insurance, warranties, permits, and compliance
10. Stakeholder and community feedback
11. Risks and opportunities
12. Priorities for the next operating year

The review should produce an action plan with owners, deadlines, budget, and success measures.

---

## 5.8.13 Adaptive Management and Optimization

Agrivoltaic systems should be managed as learning systems. Actual conditions may differ from assumptions made during feasibility and design.

Possible adaptations include:

- Changing crop varieties or planting density
- Adjusting row-specific irrigation
- Altering mowing or grazing regimes
- Adding or relocating biodiversity features
- Changing panel operating strategy where the system permits it
- Adjusting cleaning frequency based on measured soiling
- Reconfiguring monitoring thresholds
- Replacing underperforming equipment
- Improving access, storage, or safety arrangements
- Revising staffing or contractor scope

Changes should be evaluated for effects on permits, warranties, grid compliance, agricultural outcomes, and financing obligations before implementation.

## 5.8.14 Operations and Maintenance Timeline

| Frequency | PV and electrical | Agricultural | Environmental and site |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily | Dashboard and alarm review | Crop/livestock checks as required | Check severe weather or incident alerts |
| Weekly | Representative site inspection | Irrigation, access, and production review | Vegetation, drainage, and fencing check |
| Monthly | Performance and fault analysis | Production and water-use report | Environmental observation and issue review |
| Quarterly | Electrical and structural inspections | Irrigation service and machinery-access review | Erosion, habitat, and invasive-species check |
| Annually | Full service, testing, and warranty review | Yield, soil, and farm-business review | Biodiversity, soil, water, and landscape report |
| After major event | Storm, fire, fault, or impact inspection | Assess crop, livestock, and irrigation damage | Assess erosion, flooding, habitat, and pollution |
| Every 3–5 years | Deeper electrical and structural assessment | Review infrastructure and production model | Review monitoring design and objectives |

The actual schedule should follow manufacturer requirements, permits, insurance conditions, and site-specific risk.

## 5.8.15 Common O&M Risks and Mitigation

| Risk | Consequence | Early indicator | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Undetected equipment fault** | Energy loss and expensive secondary damage | Repeated alarms or unexplained underperformance | Automated alerts, daily review, rapid fault response |
| **Agricultural access conflict** | Crop damage, maintenance delay, farmer dissatisfaction | Overlapping work calendars | Shared annual and weekly access planning |
| **Irrigation failure** | Crop loss and financial impact | Low pressure, dry soil, sensor alarms | Redundancy, spare parts, regular testing |
| **Soil compaction or erosion** | Reduced productivity and water infiltration | Rutting, ponding, runoff | Controlled traffic, drainage maintenance, soil monitoring |
| **Inverter or transformer failure** | Extended generation downtime | Heat alarms, repeated trips | Preventive service, critical spares, service-level agreement |
| **Biodiversity decline** | Permit or stakeholder concerns | Habitat deterioration or invasive species | Adaptive ecological management and annual surveys |
| **Cybersecurity incident** | Loss of monitoring or unauthorized control | Unusual logins or communications | Access control, network segregation, backups, incident plan |
| **Warranty loss** | Higher repair costs | Missing maintenance or service records | Warranty register and compliant maintenance |
| **Severe weather damage** | Safety risk and prolonged outage | Storm alerts or extreme conditions | Emergency plan, inspections, insurance, spare capacity |
| **Staffing or contractor gap** | Missed maintenance and slow response | Overdue work orders or high turnover | Competency matrix, backup providers, training |

## Conclusion

Operations and Maintenance determines whether an agrivoltaic project delivers its intended energy, agricultural, environmental, and financial benefits over its operating life. The strongest O&M programmes combine reliable monitoring, preventive maintenance, fast fault response, agricultural coordination, environmental stewardship, competent staff, and disciplined record keeping.

The project should be managed as one integrated asset. PV maintenance cannot be planned without considering farm access, irrigation, soil, livestock, biodiversity, and seasonal operations. Similarly, agricultural decisions must respect electrical safety, equipment protection, grid requirements, and maintenance access.

Annual reviews and adaptive management allow the project to improve as evidence accumulates. By using measured performance rather than assumptions alone, owners and operators can reduce avoidable losses, protect the land, and maintain productive coexistence between solar generation and agriculture.
