# Section 5.5: Procurement and Contracting

## Introduction

The Procurement and Contracting phase converts the approved design into binding supplier, contractor, and service agreements. It typically runs from months 12–18, although early procurement of long-lead equipment may begin before financial close when the project has sufficient authorization and funding certainty.

The objective is to obtain the required equipment and services at a competitive, predictable cost while protecting the project from delays, defects, non-performance, and unclear responsibilities. Procurement decisions should be based on whole-life value rather than purchase price alone.

A strong procurement process should:

- Match purchases to the approved technical design
- Protect the construction and commissioning schedule
- Confirm supplier quality, warranties, and service capability
- Allocate risks to the party best able to manage them
- Preserve agricultural, environmental, and grid requirements
- Support lender, insurer, and subsidy-program requirements
- Maintain clear records for audit and future operations

> **Important:** Contract terms should be reviewed by qualified French legal, technical, tax, and insurance advisers. The templates and recommendations in this section are planning guidance, not legal advice.

## 5.5.1 Procurement Strategy

### Procurement objectives

Before requesting bids, the project team should agree on:

1. What will be purchased directly by the project owner
2. What will be included in an EPC or design-build contract
3. What will be supplied by the farmer or agricultural operator
4. Which items require long-term warranties or service agreements
5. Which risks will be retained by the owner and which transferred to contractors
6. Which packages must be ordered early to protect the schedule
7. Which technical substitutions are acceptable

### Procurement models

#### **Model A: Full EPC contract**

One contractor is responsible for engineering, procurement, construction, testing, and handover.

**Advantages:**
- Single point of responsibility
- Easier lender oversight
- Reduced owner coordination burden
- Greater schedule integration

**Disadvantages:**
- EPC pricing includes contractor risk premiums
- Less direct control over equipment selection
- Contractor financial strength becomes critical
- Agricultural and environmental requirements may receive insufficient attention unless clearly specified

**Suitable for:** Owners with limited internal construction capacity and projects with a well-defined technical scope.

#### **Model B: Multi-package procurement**

The owner contracts separately for design, civil works, electrical works, PV equipment, agricultural infrastructure, and O&M.

**Advantages:**
- Greater control over suppliers and design
- Potentially lower procurement costs
- Easier inclusion of specialist agricultural contractors
- Flexible package sequencing

**Disadvantages:**
- More interfaces and coordination responsibilities
- Owner retains more schedule and integration risk
- Greater need for technical project management
- Interface disputes may be harder to resolve

**Suitable for:** Experienced developers, cooperatives, or projects requiring highly specialized agricultural systems.

#### **Model C: Hybrid procurement**

Major PV and electrical works are contracted through an EPC or balance-of-system contractor, while the owner separately procures agricultural, environmental, monitoring, or O&M services.

**Advantages:**
- Balances responsibility and flexibility
- Allows agricultural requirements to be managed by specialist providers
- Reduces owner exposure to electrical integration risk
- Supports local and specialist suppliers

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires carefully defined interfaces
- Responsibility for delays must be explicit
- More than one contractor may require coordinated insurance and site rules

**Suitable for:** Most medium-sized agrivoltaic projects where agricultural performance is as important as energy production.

### Procurement principles

- Use competitive procurement for major packages unless there is a documented reason not to.
- Obtain at least three technically compliant bids for major contracts where the market allows.
- Evaluate suppliers on cost, quality, schedule, warranty, service, and financial strength.
- Avoid selecting equipment solely on the lowest initial price.
- Confirm that proposed substitutions preserve agricultural access, energy performance, safety, and grid compliance.
- Include the approved design, performance requirements, and project constraints in tender documents.
- Record all clarifications and issue the same information to all bidders.
- Separate technical evaluation from commercial evaluation where practical.
- Obtain lender and insurer approval before signing contracts that affect financing conditions.

## 5.5.2 Work Package Structure

Breaking the project into clear work packages helps assign responsibility, compare bids, and track cost and schedule performance.

| Package | Typical scope | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| **WP1: Development and design** | Surveys, engineering, permits, design updates | Owner/design consultant |
| **WP2: Site preparation** | Mobilization, clearing, grading, temporary facilities | Civil contractor |
| **WP3: Foundations and structures** | Piles, footings, steel, trackers, elevated structures | Structural contractor/EPC |
| **WP4: PV equipment** | Modules, mounting components, connectors, spares | Owner or EPC |
| **WP5: Electrical balance of system** | Inverters, transformers, switchgear, cables, protection | Electrical contractor/EPC |
| **WP6: Grid connection** | Substation, cable route, protection, telemetry, testing | Grid contractor/EPC/grid operator |
| **WP7: Agricultural infrastructure** | Irrigation, fencing, storage, livestock or crop infrastructure | Agricultural contractor |
| **WP8: Environmental works** | Hedgerows, habitat, drainage, erosion control, landscaping | Environmental/civil contractor |
| **WP9: Commissioning** | Testing, training, documentation, punch list | EPC/commissioning engineer |
| **WP10: Operations and maintenance** | PV O&M, agricultural operations, monitoring, reporting | O&M provider/operator |

### Interface register

For each package, define:

- Scope boundaries
- Design responsibility
- Supply responsibility
- Installation responsibility
- Testing responsibility
- Required information from other packages
- Handover documents
- Safety and access obligations
- Delay responsibility
- Warranty responsibility

A written interface register is particularly important where the PV contractor is not responsible for the agricultural system.

## 5.5.3 Equipment Procurement

### PV modules

Specify and evaluate modules using:

- Nominal power and efficiency
- Temperature coefficient
- Degradation warranty
- Product warranty and performance warranty
- Mechanical load rating
- Fire and safety classification
- Bifacial performance assumptions, if applicable
- Compatibility with mounting system and connectors
- Factory quality-control procedures
- Traceability of serial numbers
- Delivery packaging and damage procedures
- Availability of replacement modules for the operating period

Require the supplier to provide:

- Technical data sheets
- Warranty terms
- Factory inspection and test records
- Flash-test or performance-test data
- Serial-number list
- Packing list and delivery schedule
- Instructions for storage and handling

### Inverters and power electronics

Evaluate:

- Rated power and efficiency
- Grid-code compliance
- Remote control and curtailment functionality
- Monitoring and communications protocols
- Environmental operating range
- Service response times in France
- Spare-parts availability
- Warranty duration and extension options
- Replacement strategy over the project life
- Compatibility with export limitation and storage, if applicable

### Transformers and switchgear

Specify:

- Voltage ratio and rated capacity
- Short-circuit withstand
- Cooling method
- Protection and relay requirements
- Noise limits
- Fire and oil-containment measures
- Factory acceptance testing
- Delivery, lifting, and installation requirements
- Maintenance access and spare-parts arrangements

### Mounting structures and foundations

Confirm:

- Structural calculations and design loads
- Corrosion protection and material certificates
- Compatibility with the geotechnical report
- Pile or foundation testing requirements
- Tolerances and installation procedures
- Reversibility and land-restoration requirements
- Wind, snow, seismic, and agricultural machinery clearances
- Availability of replacement components

### Agricultural equipment and infrastructure

Procurement should cover more than PV equipment. It may include:

- Irrigation pumps, filters, pipes, valves, and control systems
- Water storage and rainwater management
- Fencing and gates
- Crop-support or orchard systems
- Livestock shelters and welfare infrastructure
- Storage buildings and equipment shelters
- Farm machinery or modified implements
- Soil restoration and reseeding materials
- Weather stations and agricultural sensors

Agricultural equipment should be selected with the farmer or operator, who should confirm compatibility with existing machinery, skills, maintenance capacity, and local suppliers.

## 5.5.4 Supplier Prequalification

Prequalification reduces the risk of selecting a supplier that cannot deliver the required quality, schedule, or warranty support.

### Minimum supplier information

Request:

- Company registration and ownership information
- Relevant project experience
- References for comparable agrivoltaic, PV, agricultural, or infrastructure projects
- Financial statements or evidence of financial capacity
- Insurance certificates
- Health and safety record
- Environmental and quality policies
- Manufacturing locations and capacity
- Proposed subcontractors
- Typical lead times
- Warranty and service arrangements
- Litigation, insolvency, or regulatory history where relevant

### Prequalification scoring

| Criterion | Suggested weighting |
|---|---:|
| Technical capability and relevant experience | 25% |
| Quality and warranty support | 15% |
| Schedule and manufacturing capacity | 15% |
| Financial strength | 15% |
| Price competitiveness | 15% |
| Health, safety, and environmental record | 10% |
| Local service and communication capability | 5% |

The weighting should be adjusted for the package. For a grid connection or structural package, technical competence may deserve a higher weighting than purchase price.

## 5.5.5 Tendering and Bid Evaluation

### Tender package contents

A tender package should contain:

1. Invitation to tender
2. Instructions to bidders
3. Project description and objectives
4. Site information and access constraints
5. Approved drawings and technical specifications
6. Agricultural operating requirements
7. Environmental and biodiversity requirements
8. Health, safety, and quality requirements
9. Construction schedule and milestones
10. Pricing schedule and bill of quantities
11. Form of contract
12. Insurance and bonding requirements
13. Warranty and performance requirements
14. Bid submission forms
15. Clarification process and deadline

### Tender process

1. Finalize the tender package.
2. Prequalify bidders.
3. Issue the same information to all bidders.
4. Hold a site visit or bidder briefing.
5. Receive clarification questions by a fixed deadline.
6. Issue written answers to all bidders.
7. Receive and register bids securely.
8. Complete technical evaluation.
9. Complete commercial evaluation.
10. Resolve deviations and exclusions.
11. Conduct interviews and reference checks.
12. Negotiate final terms with the preferred bidder.
13. Obtain lender, insurer, and sponsor approvals.
14. Issue a notice of award and execute the contract.

### Normalizing bids

The lowest headline price may not be the lowest evaluated cost. Normalize bids for:

- Excluded civil or electrical works
- Temporary facilities and site security
- Grid-operator fees
- Testing and commissioning
- Spare parts
- Transport, unloading, and storage
- Taxes and duties
- Warranty extensions
- O&M costs
- Contractor assumptions about soil, access, or weather
- Provisional sums
- Owner-supplied equipment

Document any commercial adjustment so the selection can be explained to lenders, investors, and auditors.

## 5.5.6 Contract Structures

### Fixed-price contract

The contractor agrees to deliver a defined scope for a stated price.

**Advantages:** Budget certainty and easier lender review.

**Risks:** Contractors may include high contingencies or pursue change orders if the scope is unclear.

**Best used when:** Design is substantially complete and site conditions are well understood.

### Guaranteed maximum price

The contractor is reimbursed for defined costs up to a maximum price, subject to agreed changes.

**Advantages:** Can accommodate remaining design development while providing a cost ceiling.

**Risks:** Requires transparent cost records and careful change management.

### Cost-plus contract

The owner pays actual allowable costs plus an agreed fee or margin.

**Advantages:** Flexible where scope or ground conditions are uncertain.

**Risks:** Greater owner cost exposure and weaker budget certainty.

**Best used when:** Early works or highly uncertain site conditions make a fixed price impractical.

### Framework or call-off agreement

The owner establishes agreed terms with a supplier for repeated purchases or services.

**Advantages:** Faster ordering and consistent terms for spares, maintenance, or multiple sites.

**Risks:** May not secure the best price for a single large purchase.

## 5.5.7 Essential Contract Terms

Every major contract should clearly define the following.

### Scope and technical requirements

- Detailed scope of work
- Contract drawings and specifications
- Order of precedence between documents
- Approved alternatives and substitution procedure
- Standards and testing requirements
- Agricultural, environmental, and access constraints
- Owner-supplied equipment and information

### Price and payment

- Contract price and currency
- Taxes and duties
- Milestone payment schedule
- Retention or holdback
- Advance-payment requirements
- Payment documentation
- Price-adjustment formula, if applicable
- Set-off rights
- Payment security or escrow arrangements

### Schedule

- Notice to proceed
- Key milestones
- Delivery dates
- Substantial completion
- Mechanical completion
- Grid connection and commissioning
- Commercial operation date
- Extension-of-time procedure
- Delay damages or agreed remedies

### Performance and acceptance

- Energy-performance requirements
- Availability or uptime requirements
- Agricultural-system performance obligations
- Grid compliance
- Test procedures
- Defect classification
- Punch-list process
- Acceptance criteria
- Re-testing requirements

### Warranty and remedies

- Product warranty
- Workmanship warranty
- Performance warranty
- Response time for defects
- Repair or replacement obligations
- Warranty start date
- Warranty exclusions
- Availability of spare parts
- Escalation process for repeated failures

### Insurance and security

- Public liability insurance
- Employers’ liability or workers’ compensation cover
- Professional indemnity insurance
- Construction all-risk insurance
- Environmental liability where relevant
- Vehicle and equipment insurance
- Performance bond
- Advance-payment bond
- Parent-company guarantee, where appropriate

### Change, suspension, and termination

- Written change-order process
- Price and schedule evaluation for changes
- Emergency work authorization
- Suspension rights
- Termination for convenience
- Termination for default
- Insolvency provisions
- Step-in rights for lenders
- Handover obligations after termination

### Disputes and governing law

- Escalation from project managers to senior representatives
- Mediation or expert determination
- Arbitration or court jurisdiction
- Governing law
- Language of the contract
- Notice requirements

### Compliance and conduct

- Health and safety
- Environmental protection
- Anti-bribery and corruption
- Modern slavery and labor standards
- Data protection and cybersecurity
- Sanctions and export controls where relevant
- Confidentiality and publicity

## 5.5.8 Performance Guarantees and Liquidated Damages

Performance guarantees should be measurable, realistic, and linked to data that can be independently verified.

### Solar performance

Possible guarantees include:

- Initial system capacity
- Specific annual yield, adjusted for agreed weather conditions
- Inverter efficiency
- System availability
- Maximum response time for faults
- Completion of commissioning tests

Avoid guarantees based on factors outside the contractor’s control unless the adjustment methodology is agreed in advance.

### Agricultural performance

Agricultural performance is influenced by weather, management, markets, pests, and farmer decisions. It is usually better managed through:

- Defined agricultural design criteria
- Minimum access and irrigation requirements
- Operator training
- Establishment and maintenance obligations
- Monitoring and reporting
- Trial periods or pilot plots
- Clearly allocated responsibility for crop selection and management

A contractor should not be given an undefined guarantee of farm revenue or yield without a robust baseline and control methodology.

### Liquidated damages

Where appropriate, contracts may define pre-agreed damages for:

- Late delivery of critical equipment
- Failure to meet agreed completion dates
- Failure to meet minimum performance after testing
- Failure to provide required documentation
- Failure to meet response-time obligations

Liquidated damages should be reviewed by legal counsel to ensure they are enforceable and proportionate under the governing law.

## 5.5.9 Long-Lead Items and Supply-Chain Management

### Typical lead-time items

| Item | Indicative lead-time range | Procurement action |
|---|---:|---|
| PV modules | 2–5 months | Reserve production capacity after financing approval |
| Inverters | 2–6 months | Confirm model, firmware, and grid compliance early |
| Transformers | 4–9 months | Order early after grid requirements are confirmed |
| Medium-voltage switchgear | 4–8 months | Coordinate with grid operator and protection engineer |
| Elevated structures | 4–8 months | Confirm structural design and manufacturing capacity |
| Steel piles or foundations | 2–5 months | Align quantities with geotechnical design |
| Irrigation equipment | 1–3 months | Coordinate with crop calendar and installation access |
| Monitoring and communications | 1–3 months | Confirm protocols and cybersecurity requirements |

Lead times vary by market conditions and project specification. The project schedule should use supplier-confirmed dates rather than generic estimates once bids are available.

### Supply-chain controls

- Confirm manufacturing capacity before contract award.
- Require monthly production and delivery updates.
- Track critical-path equipment separately.
- Approve equivalent products in writing before shipment.
- Require factory acceptance testing for critical equipment.
- Inspect packaging and shipping damage on delivery.
- Maintain a receiving register with serial numbers and quantities.
- Provide secure, dry, and suitable storage.
- Protect warranties by following manufacturer storage requirements.
- Maintain minimum critical spare parts on site.

### Delivery and logistics plan

The plan should address:

- Transport route and road restrictions
- Bridge and road-weight limits
- Delivery windows and traffic coordination
- Offloading equipment and lifting plans
- Temporary storage and security
- Weather-related access restrictions
- Packaging and waste management
- Damage inspection and claims procedure
- Delivery sequence relative to construction progress

## 5.5.10 Contractor Mobilization and Handover

Before site work begins, hold a pre-mobilization meeting covering:

- Contract scope and responsibilities
- Site rules and access
- Health, safety, and environmental plans
- Agricultural operations and no-go areas
- Environmental protection measures
- Construction schedule and reporting
- Quality inspection and hold points
- Materials approval process
- Change-order procedure
- Emergency contacts and incident reporting
- Community communication
- Temporary facilities and utilities
- Security and working hours

### Mobilization deliverables

- Approved construction method statements
- Site logistics and traffic plan
- Construction environmental management plan
- Health and safety plan
- Quality plan and inspection test plans
- Workforce induction records
- Insurance certificates and bonds
- Approved schedule and baseline budget
- Emergency response plan
- Contact and escalation list

### Handover from procurement to construction

Confirm that the construction team has received:

- Latest approved drawings
- Contract specifications
- Supplier manuals
- Delivery and storage instructions
- Inspection and test requirements
- Warranty conditions
- Contact information for supplier technical support
- Approved substitutions and deviations
- Agricultural and environmental constraints
- Current risk register

## 5.5.11 Contract Management During Construction

### Progress reporting

Require regular reporting on:

- Work completed against baseline schedule
- Cost committed and forecast final cost
- Equipment ordered, manufactured, and delivered
- Quality inspections and defects
- Safety incidents and corrective actions
- Environmental incidents and mitigation
- Agricultural disruption or access issues
- Change orders and claims
- Risks requiring owner action

### Payment verification

Payments should be linked to objectively verifiable milestones, such as:

- Approved design package
- Equipment delivery and inspection
- Foundation completion
- Structural completion
- Module installation
- Electrical completion
- Agricultural infrastructure completion
- Successful commissioning tests
- Handover documentation

Before approving payment, confirm that:

- The milestone has been achieved.
- Required inspection records are complete.
- Defects are recorded and treated appropriately.
- Required photographs or certificates are available.
- Insurance and bonds remain valid.
- No unresolved contractual notice affects payment.

### Change-order management

Every change request should state:

1. Description of the proposed change
2. Reason and initiating party
3. Technical effect
4. Agricultural and environmental effect
5. Schedule effect
6. Cost effect
7. Effect on warranties and performance guarantees
8. Effect on permits or grid approvals
9. Recommended decision
10. Written authorization before implementation

Do not allow verbal instructions to become uncontrolled scope changes. Emergency work may proceed under a defined emergency procedure, but it should be documented promptly.

### Claims management

Maintain a claims register tracking:

- Notice date
- Contract clause
- Event description
- Claimed cost
- Claimed schedule effect
- Evidence required
- Owner response
- Resolution status

Early investigation is usually less expensive than resolving a claim after completion.

## 5.5.12 Operation and Maintenance Contracting

O&M contracting should be developed before construction completion so that the operating team can influence equipment selection, spare parts, monitoring, and documentation.

### PV O&M scope

- Remote monitoring and alarm response
- Preventive inspections
- Panel and equipment cleaning where justified
- Vegetation management
- Inverter and transformer maintenance
- Electrical testing
- Thermographic inspections
- Fault diagnosis and repair
- Spare-parts management
- Warranty claims
- Performance reporting
- Emergency response

### Agricultural O&M scope

- Crop, orchard, or livestock operations
- Irrigation operation and repair
- Soil and vegetation management
- Fence and gate maintenance
- Access-road management
- Harvest and storage coordination
- Agricultural records and reporting
- Compliance with the approved agricultural plan

### O&M contract performance indicators

| KPI | Example measure |
|---|---|
| System availability | Percentage of time the PV system is available |
| Response time | Time from alarm to acknowledgment and site response |
| Fault closure | Time from fault identification to repair |
| Energy performance | Actual production versus adjusted forecast |
| Preventive maintenance | Percentage completed on schedule |
| Agricultural continuity | Planned production activities completed |
| Irrigation reliability | Percentage of required irrigation events delivered |
| Safety | Recordable incidents and corrective-action closure |
| Reporting | Reports delivered accurately and on time |

The final values should be set according to project size, technology, contract scope, and lender requirements.

## 5.5.15 Common Procurement Risks and Mitigation

| Risk | Consequence | Early indicator | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Supplier insolvency** | Equipment or deposits lost | Weak financial information or missed updates | Financial checks, payment security, performance bonds |
| **Long-lead equipment delay** | Construction and commissioning delay | Manufacturing slot not confirmed | Reserve capacity early; maintain alternative suppliers |
| **Lowest-price selection** | Quality defects and higher lifetime cost | Large unexplained price gap | Use weighted evaluation and total-cost analysis |
| **Unclear scope** | Claims and change orders | Bids contain many exclusions | Improve tender documents and normalize assumptions |
| **Unapproved substitutions** | Performance or compliance failure | Supplier proposes changes after award | Require formal technical-submittal process |
| **Transport or access failure** | Delivery damage or site delay | Route not surveyed | Complete logistics survey and delivery plan |
| **Warranty gaps** | High repair cost | Different warranties expire at different times | Maintain warranty matrix and align contract obligations |
| **Contractor underperformance** | Cost, schedule, or quality problems | Missed early milestones | Use measurable KPIs, bonds, and escalation procedures |
| **Interface disputes** | Work stoppage and claims | Responsibilities overlap or are missing | Maintain interface register and single-point coordination |
| **Agricultural disruption** | Yield loss or farmer conflict | Construction access conflicts with farm calendar | Coordinate schedule with farmer and protect access routes |
| **Environmental non-compliance** | Fines, delays, or permit breach | Unapproved clearing or runoff | Include environmental requirements in contracts and inspections |
| **Payment delay** | Supplier slowdown or lien risk | Disputed milestone evidence | Use objective milestones and prompt document review |

## Conclusion

Procurement and contracting establish the commercial foundation for construction and long-term operation. The most reliable approach is to:

- Define the work packages and interfaces clearly
- Prequalify suppliers and contractors before bidding
- Evaluate whole-life value rather than headline price
- Protect long-lead equipment and critical milestones
- Use contracts with measurable scope, performance, warranty, and change procedures
- Involve the farmer, engineer, lender, insurer, and O&M team before commitments are final
- Maintain disciplined records throughout tendering, delivery, construction, and handover

Once major contracts are signed, long-lead equipment is ordered, and the contractor is ready to mobilize, the project can move into construction and installation with a clearer allocation of cost, schedule, quality, and performance risk.
