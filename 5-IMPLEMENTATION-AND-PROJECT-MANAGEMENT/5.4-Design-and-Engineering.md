# Section 5.4: Design and Engineering

## Introduction

The Design and Engineering phase transforms the project concept into detailed, buildable drawings and specifications. This phase typically runs from months 9–15 of the project lifecycle, overlapping with feasibility studies and financing/approvals work.

A strong design is proportionate to the project scale and risk. A small pilot may require drawings that fit on 10 sheets; a 2 MW commercial project may require 50+ drawings, calculations, and specifications. The design should be:

- **Technically sound:** All components are appropriately sized, sequenced, and protected
- **Constructible:** Contractor and suppliers can execute from the drawings without ambiguity
- **Operationally efficient:** Maintenance access, monitoring, and spare parts are planned
- **Environmentally responsible:** Habitats are protected, drainage is managed, and visual impact is minimized
- **Financially accurate:** Cost and schedule assumptions are defensible to lenders and subsidy programs
- **Compliant:** All relevant regulations, standards, and grid-operator requirements are met

This section covers the scope of design work, key deliverables, quality-control processes, and coordination requirements.

> **Important:** This section describes the design process framework. Detailed technical design standards and specifications should be prepared by qualified French engineers (with appropriate professional registration and insurance) and should comply with applicable French regulations, European standards, and grid-operator requirements.

## 5.4.1 Design Scope and Sequencing

### Design disciplines

A complete agrivoltaic project design requires contributions from multiple disciplines:

| Discipline | Focus | Typical deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| **Solar/electrical engineer** | Array layout, inverters, transformers, cabling, grounding, protection | Single-line diagrams, electrical specifications, load-flow analysis |
| **Structural/civil engineer** | Foundations, mounting structure, access roads, drainage | Structural calculations, geotechnical drawings, drainage plans |
| **Agricultural engineer** | Crop compatibility, irrigation, equipment access, soil management | Agricultural operating plan, irrigation design, access route map |
| **Environmental specialist** | Habitats, landscaping, biodiversity measures, stormwater | Environmental specifications, habitat design, monitoring plan |
| **Quantity surveyor/estimator** | Bills of materials, cost estimates, procurement strategy | BOM, budget by work package, cost justification |
| **QHSE (Quality, Health, Safety, Environment) specialist** | Construction safety, environmental protection, waste management | QHSE plan, site management procedures |

### Design phases

#### **Phase 1: Preliminary/Concept Design (Weeks 1–4)**

**Objective:** Establish feasibility of one or more design concepts and test key assumptions

**Typical scope:**
- Preliminary array layout (footprint, row spacing, height)
- Estimated equipment quantities (panels, inverters, transformers)
- Preliminary cost estimate (±30% accuracy)
- Preliminary schedule estimate
- Identification of major design challenges
- Decision points (e.g., fixed vs. tracking, elevated vs. ground-level)

**Deliverables:**
- Sketch layouts and 3D visualization
- Preliminary equipment list
- Preliminary cost and schedule

**Review:** Internal design team and project sponsor

**Duration:** 2–4 weeks

#### **Phase 2: Developed Design (Weeks 4–12)**

**Objective:** Develop the preferred concept into a design that can be used for contractor bidding and early procurement

**Typical scope:**
- Detailed array layout with final row spacing, height, orientation
- Equipment specifications (panels, inverters, transformers, mounting system)
- Electrical one-line diagram and protection scheme
- Preliminary structural calculations
- Agricultural operating procedures
- Environmental and biodiversity specifications
- Drainage and stormwater management design
- Cost estimate (±15% accuracy)
- Schedule with procurement lead times

**Deliverables:**
- Site plan (scale 1:500 or 1:1000)
- Electrical single-line diagram
- 3D rendering for landscape visualization
- Preliminary structural calculations
- Equipment specifications and data sheets
- BOM with equipment costs
- Budget breakdown by work package
- Agricultural operation manual (draft)
- Environmental specifications (draft)

**Review:** Design review with contractor, lender, grid operator, and environmental advisor

**Duration:** 6–8 weeks

#### **Phase 3: Detailed Design (Weeks 12–20)**

**Objective:** Complete all information needed for construction contracting and procurement

**Typical scope:**
- Complete structural calculations and drawings
- Foundation design and calculations
- Electrical calculations (cable sizing, voltage drop, grounding resistance, protection coordination)
- Detailed construction drawings for each work package
- Complete specifications for all equipment and materials
- Detailed bills of materials
- Detailed cost estimates (±10% accuracy)
- Final equipment procurement schedule
- Construction schedule (logic and resource plan)
- Quality assurance and inspection procedures
- Environmental and safety management procedures

**Deliverables:**
- Foundation plan and details (scale 1:100)
- Electrical design package (single-line diagram, protective device calculations, cable tray layout)
- Civil and structural drawings (roads, drainage, site layout)
- Equipment installation details
- Cable routing and conduit schedule
- Detailed specifications (pages 50–100+)
- Complete BOM with part numbers and suppliers
- Final budget (±10% accuracy)
- Construction schedule (Gantt chart)
- Quality and health-safety plan

**Review:** Design sign-off by engineer, lender, and grid operator

**Duration:** 8–12 weeks (can overlap with procurement and early construction preparation)

### Design input and assumptions documentation

Throughout design, maintain a register of key assumptions:

| Assumption | Source | Confidence | Risk if wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Solar resource** | PVGIS data for site location | Medium (historical data) | ±5% energy yield impact |
| **Panel efficiency** | Current manufacturer spec | High | Minimal (spec-driven) |
| **Soil bearing capacity** | Geotechnical investigation | Medium (site-specific) | Foundation cost impact |
| **Wind loading** | Eurocode / French standards | High (regulatory) | Structural cost impact |
| **Shade loss** | 3D model of surroundings | Medium (depends on precision) | ±2–5% yield impact |
| **Agricultural clearance** | Equipment manufacturer spec | High (known) | Operational constraint |
| **Grid voltage and protection** | Grid operator study | Medium (may change) | Protection system cost |
| **Environmental constraints** | Baseline survey and screening | Medium (seasonal variation) | Mitigation cost/timing impact |
| **Construction access** | Site visit and road survey | Medium (weather-dependent) | Schedule risk |
| **Labor availability** | Local contractor feedback | Low (market-dependent) | Cost and schedule risk |

## 5.4.2 Solar PV System Design

### Array layout and orientation

#### **Fixed tilt systems**

**Characteristics:**
- Panels mounted on fixed-angle aluminum or steel frames
- Angle optimized for site latitude and solar resource (typically 30–35° in France)
- Rows spaced to avoid excessive shade (spacing depends on height and latitude)
- Annual energy yield: baseline reference

**Layout considerations:**
- **Row spacing (east-west):** Typically 4–6 m center-to-center (depends on panel height and latitude)
- **Panel orientation:** South-facing is standard; east-west options possible (reduced output but better seasonal balance)
- **Shade modeling:** Use 3D design software (e.g., PVsyst, Helioscope) to quantify shade losses at different times of year
- **Mechanical access:** Leave 1.5–2 m corridors for maintenance vehicles

**Energy output:** 100% (baseline case for comparison)

**Cost:** €400–500/kW (panels + mounting system)

#### **Single-axis tracking systems**

**Characteristics:**
- Panels rotate on an east-west horizontal axis to track the sun throughout the day
- Typically 20–30% higher output than fixed systems
- More complex mechanically and electrically
- Higher O&M cost (motors, sensors, cleaning harder to automate)

**Layout considerations:**
- **Row spacing:** Requires wider spacing (8–12 m) than fixed systems to minimize row-to-row shade
- **Tracker control:** Sun-tracking algorithm or manual seasonal adjustment (depends on system)
- **Wind exposure:** Exposed trackers may require higher foundations; wind bracing essential

**Energy output:** 120–130% of fixed system (in French climate)

**Cost:** €550–700/kW (panels + tracking system + controls)

**Agricultural compatibility:** Typically moderate to poor (shadows move throughout day; difficult to predict crop performance; access harder)

#### **Dynamic/elevated agrivoltaic systems**

**Characteristics:**
- Panels mounted high above ground (3–4 m+) on elevated platforms
- Allows full-height agricultural machinery and livestock to operate below
- More sophisticated structurally; requires specialized engineering
- May include dynamic adjustment or bifacial capabilities

**Energy output:** Similar to fixed systems on ground, but varies by elevation and bifacial design

**Cost:** €600–900/kW (higher structural and electrical complexity)

**Agricultural compatibility:** Excellent (unrestricted ground-level operations)

#### **Crop-integrated systems (emerging)**

**Characteristics:**
- Panels integrated into crop canopy or orchard framework
- Low-height systems (1.5–3 m) suitable for specific crops (berries, asparagus, orchards)
- Experimental technology; limited commercial deployment
- Requires specialized design and agricultural integration

**Cost:** €700–1000/kW (custom design, specialized components)

**Status:** Pilot projects only; not recommended for commercial deployment without specialized expertise

### Module and inverter selection

#### **PV modules**

**Types:**
- **Monocrystalline (c-Si):** Industry standard, 18–22% efficiency, €0.12–0.18/Wc
- **Polycrystalline (c-Si):** Older standard, 16–19% efficiency, €0.10–0.15/Wc
- **Bifacial:** Higher efficiency (20–22%), captures rear-side irradiance, premium cost (+€0.02–0.05/Wc)
- **Thin-film (CdTe, CIGS):** Emerging segment, lower efficiency (12–17%), better temperature and low-light performance

**Selection criteria:**
- **Efficiency:** Higher efficiency = smaller array footprint = lower land impact
- **Temperature coefficient:** Lower coefficient = better performance in high-temperature climates
- **Durability:** Ensure 25-year warranty and proven track record
- **Supply chain:** Avoid single-source supplier; confirm multi-year price stability
- **Bifacial suitability:** If reflective ground coverage is planned (e.g., white gravel, mirrors), bifacial modules add 5–10% output

**Sizing:** Oversize array by 10–20% relative to inverter capacity (allows partial clipping without excessive curtailment)

**Layout:** Typically 8–12 modules per series string (string voltage in range 250–550 V); multiple strings combined at combiner box before inverter

#### **Inverters**

**Types:**
- **String inverters:** Each string has dedicated inverter; 30–100 kW typical size; modular architecture
- **Central inverter:** Single large inverter (500 kW–2 MW); lower cost per kW but single point of failure
- **Module-level power electronics (MLPE):** Microinverters on each module or power optimizers on each string

**Selection criteria:**
- **Efficiency:** >98% AC efficiency (lower losses)
- **Clipping:** Design to avoid excessive power clipping (oversizing array by 10–20% is typical; mild clipping acceptable)
- **Curtailment capability:** Inverter must support remote curtailment command from grid operator
- **Monitoring:** Integrated data-logging and remote monitoring via internet/4G
- **Grid connection:** Supports anti-islanding and grid fault response per French standard (NF C 15-712)
- **Redundancy:** Consider two smaller inverters instead of one large (improves availability)
- **Repair/replacement:** Confirm availability of spare parts and service support in France

**Sizing:** Typically 0.8–1.0 inverter capacity per array capacity (e.g., 1.0 MW array might have 900 kW inverter capacity to allow some clipping)

### Electrical system design

#### **One-line diagram and protection scheme**

The electrical single-line diagram shows:
- PV array and DC combiner box
- Inverter(s)
- Disconnects and switching devices
- Transformer (if required for voltage step-up)
- Protection devices (surge arrestors, fuses, circuit breakers, overcurrent relays)
- Grid connection point

**Protection requirements:**
- **DC side:** Surge protection (Type 2 arrestor), DC combiner with fuses or breakers
- **AC side:** Grid-fault protection (RCD Type A, Type B, or other per standard), overcurrent protection, overvoltage suppression
- **Islanding prevention:** Inverter anti-islanding function + grid disconnection device
- **Monitoring and control:** Remote on/off capability, export limit enforcement, data logging

#### **Cable sizing and voltage drop**

**DC cables (array to inverter):**
- Size cables to limit voltage drop to <3% (per standard)
- Typical cable size: 6–35 mm² (depends on string current and length)
- Use UV-resistant, temperature-rated cables (e.g., PV cables per IEC 60811-401)

**AC cables (inverter to grid):**
- Size cables to limit voltage drop to <3%
- Typical cable size: 50–240 mm² (depends on inverter power and distance to connection point)
- Single-phase or three-phase depending on inverter output and grid service

**Calculation method:**
- Voltage drop (V) = (Resistance per unit length × Length × Current) / 1000
- Target: Keep V < 3% of nominal voltage (DC: 600 V max; AC: 400 V nominal)

#### **Grounding system**

- **DC array grounding:** Center-point grounding to limit leakage current
- **AC system grounding:** Neutral grounding at transformer or inverter
- **Lightning protection:** Grounding rod at service entrance; surge protection on all external circuits
- **Continuity:** All metal structural components bonded to common grounding electrode

**Grounding electrode resistance:** Target <10 ohms (verify with soil resistivity testing if required)

### Transformer sizing (if required)

**Requirement:** Step up inverter voltage from 400 V to grid connection voltage (typically 10–20 kV)

**Sizing:**
- Transformer capacity = Inverter output power (+ 10% margin)
- Example: 1.0 MW inverter output → 1.1 MVA transformer
- Efficiency: >98.5% (oil-cooled transformer typical for agrivoltaic)

**Location:** At the project substation, near grid connection point

**Cooling:** Oil-cooled transformer typical; air-cooled options available but less common for this power range

**Noise:** Confirm compliance with local noise limits (transformers produce audible hum at 50 Hz and harmonics)

## 5.4.3 Agricultural System Design

### Crop layout and clearance requirements

#### **Crop compatibility survey**

Before finalizing PV layout, confirm:
- **Minimum clearance:** Distance from panel bottom to ground or crop canopy
- **Shade tolerance:** Selected crops' yield loss under projected shade levels
- **Mechanical access:** Whether tractors and harvesters can operate beneath/between panels
- **Irrigation:** How water reaches crop areas (rain interception under panels; supplemental irrigation needed?)

#### **Layout design**

**Example for shade-tolerant crop (e.g., asparagus, mushrooms, forage):**
- Rows 5–6 m apart (center-to-center)
- 2 m working width beneath panels
- Ground clearance: 2.0–2.5 m minimum

**Example for elevated agrivoltaic (e.g., orchard or small livestock):**
- Rows 8–12 m apart
- Full-height clearance (3.5+ m) for machinery or livestock
- Minimal shade impact on canopy

**Documentation:**
- Site plan showing panel rows and crop zones
- Cross-section showing heights and clearances
- Machinery access routes and turning areas

### Irrigation system design (if required)

#### **Water requirement assessment**

- **Current irrigation demand:** Annual water requirement for current crop (from farm records)
- **PV shade impact:** Estimated change in evapotranspiration (typically 10–20% reduction under panels due to lower ambient temperature and wind)
- **Rainfall:** Annual precipitation at site (regional climate data)
- **Net irrigation need:** (Crop requirement − Rainfall − Shade benefit) × 0.85 (typical efficiency)

#### **Irrigation design**

**System options:**
- **Drip irrigation:** Water directly to plant roots; low water waste; suitable for row crops, orchards
- **Sprinkler:** Overhead spray; higher water waste; good for forage; incompatible with panels
- **Micro-sprinkler:** Compromise between drip and overhead; some higher evaporation
- **Flood irrigation:** Least efficient; rarely suitable for agrivoltaic

**Layout:**
- Drip lines or micro-sprinklers along crop rows
- Main supply line (typically 25–50 mm diameter PVC or poly pipe) running along panel rows or access road
- Water storage reservoir (if site has seasonal water stress)
- Filtration system (for water quality; essential for drip systems)

**Water source:**
- Existing well or borehole
- Rainwater harvest (from panel wash-off during rain events)
- Mains water supply
- River or stream abstraction (requires environmental and water-rights assessment)

**Durability:**
- Poly pipe and drip systems last 5–10 years; budget replacement into O&M plan
- Main water lines (PVC or buried poly) last 20+ years

### Equipment storage and access

#### **Working area requirements**

- **Access routes:** 3.5 m minimum width for tractors (wider for combines, forage harvesters)
- **Turning areas:** 30–50 m diameter areas for machinery maneuvers
- **Loading zones:** Flat area for harvest loading or equipment staging
- **Storage:** Covered or semi-covered structure for equipment, fuel, fertilizer (200–500 m² typical)

#### **Layout strategy**

- Avoid fragmenting agricultural area with infrastructure
- Locate storage and loading outside of main production zones
- Plan for seasonal access restrictions (e.g., wet-weather soil protection)

## 5.4.4 Structural and Geotechnical Design

### Geotechnical investigation

**Investigation scope:**
- Soil boring at multiple locations (typically 5–10 boreholes for a 1.5 MW site)
- Standard Penetration Test (SPT) or Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP) testing
- Laboratory testing of soil samples (grain size, bearing capacity, compressibility)
- Depth to bedrock and groundwater table

**Cost:** €3,000–8,000 typical

**Deliverables:**
- Geotechnical report with soil classification, bearing capacity estimates, and settlement predictions
- Recommendations for foundation type and depth

### Foundation design

#### **Foundation options**

| Foundation type | Suitable soil | Installation | Cost (per pile) | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Driven steel pile** | Sand, gravel, mixed | Pile driver + crane | €100–200/m depth | Simple; proven; quick | Equipment-dependent |
| **Screw pile** | Sand, clay, mixed | Screw-in machine | €120–250/m depth | No vibration; versatile | Soil-dependent; slower |
| **Concrete pad** | Stiff clay, weak rock | Excavation + concrete | €80–150/m depth | Simple; low-tech | Requires bearing capacity; settlement risk |
| **Micropile** | Rock, weak soil | Drilling + injection | €150–300/m depth | Works in difficult soil | Expensive; specialized |
| **Anchored riser** | Shallow rock, bedrock | Bolting + grout | €200–400/anchor | Minimal excavation | Limited applicability |

**Typical choice for agrivoltaic:** Driven steel piles or screw piles (reversible; minimal ground disturbance; compatible with agriculture)

**Design parameters:**
- **Depth:** Typically 1.5–3.0 m (depends on soil bearing capacity, frost penetration, and uplift wind loads)
- **Spacing:** 4–6 m typical (module width)
- **Lateral bracing:** Cross-bracing or perimeter bonding between piles for wind resistance

### Structural calculations

**Loading analysis:**
- **Dead load:** Weight of PV panels, mounting structure, cables, combiner boxes
- **Live load:** Maintenance personnel (250 kg concentrated load per 2 m²)
- **Wind load:** Per Eurocode EN 1991-1-4 (French wind map; site-specific altitude and exposure factors)
- **Snow load:** Per Eurocode EN 1991-1-3 (French climate zone; roof-like surfaces may accumulate snow)
- **Seismic:** Per Eurocode EN 1998 (if in seismic zone; most of France is low-risk)
- **Combination factors:** Wind + dead load (max); snow + dead load + 50% wind (typical)

**Output:**
- Structural calculations (engineer's stamp required)
- Foundation design drawings (section, plan, details)
- Bill of materials for structural steel
- Installation manual and inspection checklist

### Access roads and drainage

#### **Access roads**

- **Width:** 3.5–4.5 m (single lane with passing areas every 50 m)
- **Surface:** Compacted gravel or asphalt (depending on traffic intensity and season)
- **Maintenance:** Regular grading and pothole repair
- **Drainage:** Side ditches or crowned profile to shed water

**Cost:** €20–50/m typical (materials and labor)

#### **Site drainage**

- **Surface runoff:** Prevent concentration of flow; use swales or ditches to disperse
- **Panel drip-line:** Concentrated water discharge at panel edges; manage with level changes or vegetation
- **Compaction:** Construction traffic compacts soil; plan for decompaction and re-profiling post-construction
- **Erosion control:** Seeding, mulch, or erosion-control blankets on slopes >5%

## 5.4.5 Environmental and Biodiversity Integration Design

### Habitat protection and enhancement

#### **Protection measures**

- **Hedgerows and tree lines:** Exclude from construction area; buffer zone ≥5 m from panel edge
- **Wetlands and watercourses:** Maintain 10–30 m riparian buffer (depends on site sensitivity)
- **Nesting and denning areas:** Identify and protect during construction and operation
- **Access restrictions:** Fencing to exclude public access and limit disturbance

#### **Enhancement measures**

- **Wildflower strips:** Sown along panel rows or access roads; 1–2 m width; native species
- **Hedgerow planting:** If existing hedges are thin or damaged; native species; 5–10 m sections typical
- **Ponds or swales:** For water retention and biodiversity (frogs, dragonflies, birds)
- **Dead wood piles:** Habitat for invertebrates and small mammals
- **Bird and bat boxes:** Mounted on poles or existing structures
- **Grazing management:** Rotational or continuous grazing (sheep under panels) for vegetation control and habitat management

**Cost:** €500–2,000/hectare typical for enhancement measures

### Landscape and visual integration

#### **Visual impact assessment**

**Questions to address:**
- Who views the site? (Neighbors, travelers on local roads, recreational users)
- From what distance and direction are views? (Use viewpoint maps)
- What is the existing landscape character? (Open field, wooded, mixed)
- Will the PV system be prominent or blend in?
- Are there existing screen features (hedges, buildings, topography)?

#### **Mitigation measures**

- **Landform:** Use site topography to screen panels (locate on valley floors or behind ridges)
- **Vegetation screening:** Hedgerow planting or existing tree buffer
- **Panel coloring:** Standard blue/black panels reduce visibility vs. white or reflective (though reflectivity is poor for energy)
- **Access structure:** Keep fencing, gates, and buildings visually recessive
- **Maintenance:** Regular vegetation management to maintain visual quality

### Monitoring and management

#### **Baseline monitoring**

Before construction, establish baselines for:
- **Biodiversity:** Bird and bat activity, invertebrate populations, plant communities
- **Soil:** Texture, compaction, organic matter, pH, nutrient levels
- **Water:** Groundwater depth, surface runoff velocity, drainage patterns
- **Landscape:** Photographs from key viewpoints

**Methods:**
- Visual surveys (field walks, species counts)
- Sampling (soil pits, water quality sampling)
- Instrumentation (water level monitoring, runoff measurement)

#### **Operational monitoring**

**Annual monitoring program:**
- Bird and bat activity (point counts, acoustic monitoring)
- Vegetation surveys (plant communities, weed pressure)
- Soil condition (compaction tests, organic matter loss)
- Water management (ponding, drainage adequacy)
- Landscape condition (erosion, vegetation health)

**Cost:** €2,000–5,000/year typical

**Reporting:** Annual report to local environmental regulator (if required by approval conditions)

## 5.4.6 Design Review and Approval Process

### Design review schedule

| Milestone | Reviewer | Focus | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preliminary design review** | Internal team, sponsor | Feasibility, concept approval | Week 4 |
| **Developed design review** | Contractor, lender, grid operator | Buildability, cost, schedule | Week 10 |
| **Detailed design review** | Engineer certification, authority approval | Compliance, safety, environmental | Week 18 |
| **Final design sign-off** | Professional engineer, sponsor | Authority to construct | Week 20 |

### Approval sign-offs required

- [ ] Design engineer (responsible engineer; French registration required)
- [ ] Structural engineer (for foundation and mounting structure)
- [ ] Electrical engineer (for grid connection compliance)
- [ ] Grid operator (for electrical protection and telemetry)
- [ ] Environmental compliance (if EIA was required)
- [ ] Lender technical team
- [ ] Contractor (for constructability and cost)
- [ ] Health and Safety coordinator (French QHSE regulations)

### Design document control

Maintain version control:
- Issue design documents with date, version number, and revision history
- Track comments and changes from reviewers
- Maintain a log of approvals and sign-offs
- Keep archive of all versions (not just latest)

## 5.4.7 Cost Estimation and Budget Refinement

### Detailed cost breakdown

| Cost category | 1.5 MW project typical | Unit | % of CapEx |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PV modules** | €900,000–1,100,000 | €/kW = 600–740 | 30–37% |
| **Mounting structure & civil** | €600,000–800,000 | €/kW = 400–530 | 20–27% |
| **Inverters, transformers, electrical** | €300,000–450,000 | €/kW = 200–300 | 10–15% |
| **Agricultural systems** | €200,000–400,000 | Varies | 7–13% |
| **Grid connection & infrastructure** | €150,000–300,000 | Varies | 5–10% |
| **Soft costs (design, permits, etc.)** | €150,000–250,000 | Varies | 5–8% |
| **Contingency (10–15%)** | €240,000–420,000 | Varies | 8–14% |
| **Total CapEx** | €2,540,000–3,720,000 | €/kW = 1,690–2,480 | 100% |

### Cost estimate accuracy

| Design phase | Estimate accuracy | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Feasibility study | ±50% | Low |
| Preliminary design | ±30% | Medium |
| Developed design | ±15% | Medium-high |
| Detailed design | ±10% | High |
| Contractor bids received | ±5% | Very high |

**Strategy:** Use conservative assumptions (high end of cost range) in detailed estimate; use actual quotes from suppliers and contractors where possible

### Risk-adjusted budget

Add contingency reserves:
- **Design contingency:** 5–10% (for unforeseen design issues)
- **Construction contingency:** 5–10% (for ground conditions, weather, inflation)
- **Professional services contingency:** 10–15% (for additional studies, design changes)
- **Operational contingency:** 2–3% (for early maintenance or replacement items)

**Total contingency:** 10–15% of base CapEx typical

**Reserve strategy:** Separate contingency from base budget; use only with approval; track and report monthly

## 5.4.8 Schedule Development

### Design schedule

| Task | Duration | Dependencies | Resources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preliminary design** | 2–4 weeks | Feasibility complete | 0.5 FTE engineer |
| **Geotechnical investigation** | 1–2 weeks field; 2–3 weeks reporting | Site access confirmed | External specialist |
| **Developed design** | 4–6 weeks | Preliminary design; geotech report | 1.0 FTE engineer + specialist |
| **Contractor pre-bid consultation** | 1–2 weeks | Developed design | 0.5 FTE engineer |
| **Detailed design** | 4–8 weeks | Contractor feedback | 1.0–1.5 FTE engineer + specialist |
| **Grid operator coordination** | Parallel; 1–2 weeks input | Electrical design draft | 0.25 FTE |
| **Permitting coordination** | Parallel; 1–2 weeks input | Planning and environmental info | 0.25 FTE |
| **Design review and approval** | 1–2 weeks | Detailed design complete | 0.5 FTE |

**Critical path:** Geotechnical investigation (if on the critical path) → Detailed design → Approvals

**Compression opportunities:**
- Geotech investigation can start before feasibility complete (reduce schedule by 4–6 weeks)
- Developed design can proceed with preliminary geotech results (increase risk slightly)
- Grid operator coordination can start after preliminary electrical design (reduce schedule by 1–2 weeks)

## 5.4.9 Quality Assurance and Quality Control

### QA/QC plan

**Quality objectives:**
- All drawings and specifications are complete and accurate
- All designs comply with applicable regulations and standards
- Cost estimates are defensible and within lender requirements
- Schedule is realistic and achievable

**QA/QC activities:**
- Design review meetings (weekly during design development)
- Checklist review (all drawings, all specifications, all calculations)
- Cross-discipline coordination (electrical + structural, for example)
- Specialist review (structural calculations by second engineer)
- Regulatory compliance check (grid operator, planning, environmental)
- Lender review (for financial reasonableness)

### Document control and archive

Maintain organized project file with:
- Design calculations (PDF, with engineer stamp)
- Drawings (PDF, with revision number and date)
- Specifications (technical data sheets, equipment cut sheets)
- Bills of materials
- Cost estimates and budget tracking
- Correspondence with all approvers and reviewers
- Meeting notes and decision records
- Change logs (if design changes occur)

**Storage:** Secure digital archive (cloud or company server) with version control and backup

## 5.4.10 Coordination with Parallel Workstreams

### Financing coordination

**Lender requirements:**
- Lender typically appoints independent engineer to review design
- Lender may require cost certifications or guarantees
- Lender may restrict equipment choices (must be on approved list)
- Lender may require performance guarantees (e.g., solar output, equipment uptime)

**Timeline:** Developed design review (week 10) → Independent engineer feedback (week 11–12) → Lender approval (week 14)

### Permitting coordination

**Planning authority requirements:**
- May require revised landscaping or visual mitigation measures
- May impose conditions on construction timing (e.g., no construction during nesting season)
- May require design modifications for drainage or traffic

**Timeline:** Developed design (week 10) → Planning authority feedback (if required) (week 11–14) → Design adjustment (week 14–16) → Final approval (week 18–20)

### Grid connection coordination

**Grid operator requirements:**
- May require protection system modifications
- May require telemetry or control equipment modifications
- May impose export limit or curtailment conditions

**Timeline:** Preliminary electrical design (week 6–8) → Grid operator feedback (week 9–12) → Design refinement (week 12–16) → Final grid approval (week 18)

## 5.4.12 Common Design Risks and Mitigation

| Risk | Consequence | Early indicator | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Design delays** | Schedule slip of 2–4 weeks | Designer understaffed or missing information | Assign experienced team; confirm information early |
| **Geotech surprises** | Foundation cost increase 20–40% | Poor boring coverage or interpretation | Additional boreholes; specialist review |
| **Equipment availability** | Procurement delay 4–8 weeks | Supplier long-lead or discontinued items | Confirm availability before specifying |
| **Grid operator requirements change** | Electrical redesign; cost increase 10–20% | Slow grid response; late feedback | Early engagement; written confirmation |
| **Planning or EIA delays** | Schedule slip 2–6 weeks | Late permitting feedback; design conflicts | Early consultation; responsive design changes |
| **Agricultural incompatibility** | Crop failure or yield loss 20–50% | Farmer concerns; pilot data unclear | Co-design with farmer; conservative shade assumptions |
| **Cost estimate uncertainty** | Budget increase 10–20% | Sparse competitive quotes; one supplier | Obtain 2–3 quotes per major item; build contingency |
| **Constructability issues** | Construction cost increase 10–15%; schedule slip 4–8 weeks | Contractor concerns during design review | Involve contractor early; simplify complex details |
| **Environmental conflicts** | Design delay or redesign; permit conditions | Baseline survey identifies sensitive features | Early environmental survey; design constraints |
| **Standards or regulation changes** | Design rework; cost increase 5–15% | Regulatory update during design | Monitor regulatory landscape; flexibility in design |

## Conclusion

The Design and Engineering phase transforms a feasible concept into a buildable, operationally sound, and compliant project. Strong design requires:

- **Interdisciplinary teamwork** (solar, structural, electrical, agricultural, environmental expertise)
- **Early stakeholder coordination** (lender, grid operator, planning authority, farmer/operator)
- **Quality discipline** (thorough review, approval sign-offs, change management)
- **Conservative assumptions** (cost, schedule, performance)
- **Proportionate documentation** (appropriate level of detail for project scale and risk)

The cost of design (typically 3–5% of total project cost) is small compared to the cost of poor design discovered during construction (rework, delay, cost overruns). Investing in thorough, coordinated design up front is one of the best uses of project resources.

Once detailed design is complete and approved by all stakeholders, the project is ready to proceed to procurement and construction.
