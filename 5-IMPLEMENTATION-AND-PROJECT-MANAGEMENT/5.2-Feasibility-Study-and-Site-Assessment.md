# Section 5.2: Feasibility Study and Site Assessment

## Introduction

The feasibility study is the evidence-building stage of an agrivoltaic project. Its purpose is to determine whether a proposed site can support agricultural production, solar generation, environmental objectives, grid connection, regulatory approval, and an acceptable financial return at the same time.

A strong feasibility study does not begin with a preferred panel layout. It begins with the site, the agricultural system, and the project objectives. The assessment should identify constraints early, test alternative configurations, quantify uncertainty, and provide a documented basis for a go/no-go decision.

The study should be proportionate to the project scale and risk. A small pilot may require a focused assessment, while a commercial project may require seasonal field surveys, detailed engineering, formal environmental studies, agricultural references, and grid studies.

> **Important:** This section provides a planning framework. Site-specific legal, environmental, agricultural, grid, and permitting requirements should be confirmed with qualified French professionals and the relevant authorities before a project decision is made.

---

## 5.2.1 Objectives and Scope of the Feasibility Study

### Primary objectives

The feasibility study should answer five questions:

1. **Can the site support the proposed agricultural activity?**
2. **Can the site support a technically viable and safe PV system?**
3. **Can the agricultural and energy systems coexist without unacceptable impacts?**
4. **Can the project obtain the required permissions and grid connection?**
5. **Can the project be financed and operated at an acceptable risk level?**

### Minimum study scope

| Assessment area | Core question | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| Land and tenure | Can the project legally and practically use the land? | Land-control and title summary |
| Solar resource | Is the solar resource sufficient and predictable? | Preliminary energy model |
| Agriculture | Can production remain viable under the proposed system? | Agricultural baseline and operating concept |
| Environment | What ecological constraints or opportunities exist? | Environmental constraints map |
| Hydrology and soil | Will the project alter water movement or soil function? | Soil and water baseline |
| Grid | Can electricity be connected at an acceptable cost and date? | Grid connection assessment |
| Planning and permits | Is the development pathway realistic? | Permitting route and risk register |
| Economics | Can the project generate an acceptable return? | Preliminary financial model |
| Stakeholders | Is there sufficient support to continue? | Engagement record and issues log |

### Study boundaries

At the beginning of the study, document:

- Site boundaries and parcels included
- Proposed project capacity range
- Agricultural production objectives
- Target commissioning date
- Expected project lifetime
- Land ownership and lease assumptions
- Grid export or self-consumption assumptions
- Environmental and biodiversity objectives
- Decision-makers and approval authorities
- Information that is known, estimated, or still missing

## 5.2.2 Site Evaluation Checklist

The following checklist is intended for an initial screening visit and desktop review. Items marked **critical** should be resolved before committing substantial development expenditure.

### A. Land, tenure, and access

- [ ] Cadastral parcels identified and mapped
- [ ] Ownership confirmed for every relevant parcel
- [ ] Lease, easement, or option agreement pathway understood
- [ ] Existing agricultural tenancy or usage rights identified
- [ ] Existing rights of way and servitudes mapped
- [ ] Legal access available for construction and operations
- [ ] Heavy-vehicle access assessed
- [ ] Land fragmentation and parcel boundaries documented
- [ ] Existing buildings, wells, drainage, fences, and utilities mapped
- [ ] Decommissioning and land-restoration obligations considered

**Critical red flags:** unresolved ownership, disputed access, incompatible tenancy rights, or inability to bring construction equipment to the site.

### B. Solar and physical conditions

- [ ] Solar resource screened using a reliable long-term dataset
- [ ] Topography and slope mapped
- [ ] Horizon shading and nearby obstructions assessed
- [ ] Orientation and row-spacing options considered
- [ ] Wind, snow, and extreme-weather exposure considered
- [ ] Flood, erosion, and landslide risks screened
- [ ] Geotechnical conditions reviewed at a preliminary level
- [ ] Ground conditions suitable for the proposed foundations assessed
- [ ] Construction season constraints identified

### C. Agricultural conditions

- [ ] Current land use and production history documented
- [ ] Crop, orchard, livestock, or mixed-use system identified
- [ ] Soil type, depth, structure, pH, organic matter, and fertility reviewed
- [ ] Drainage and compaction risks assessed
- [ ] Irrigation source, rights, capacity, and reliability confirmed
- [ ] Existing farm machinery and working widths documented
- [ ] Turning areas and access lanes protected in the concept layout
- [ ] Shade, rain interception, and microclimate effects considered
- [ ] Yield baseline established from local records or comparable fields
- [ ] Labour, storage, harvesting, and market requirements documented
- [ ] Agricultural continuity during construction considered

### D. Environmental and ecological conditions

- [ ] Protected areas and ecological designations screened
- [ ] Habitats and landscape features mapped
- [ ] Hedgerows, trees, wetlands, ponds, streams, and riparian zones identified
- [ ] Protected or sensitive species risks screened
- [ ] Bird and bat activity considered where relevant
- [ ] Existing biodiversity value documented before design decisions
- [ ] Landscape and visual sensitivity assessed
- [ ] Noise, lighting, and construction disturbance considered
- [ ] Invasive-species risks assessed
- [ ] Biodiversity enhancement opportunities identified

### E. Water and soil protection

- [ ] Surface-water flow paths mapped
- [ ] Flooding and waterlogging history documented
- [ ] Groundwater sensitivity screened
- [ ] Existing drainage infrastructure mapped
- [ ] Runoff, erosion, and sediment-control needs identified
- [ ] Panel drip-line and concentrated-flow risks considered
- [ ] Soil sealing and impervious-surface areas minimized
- [ ] Water quality protection measures identified

### F. Grid and infrastructure

- [ ] Nearest substations and potential connection points identified
- [ ] Preliminary export capacity checked with the network operator
- [ ] Distance to connection point measured
- [ ] Route for cables and access infrastructure assessed
- [ ] Voltage level and protection requirements considered
- [ ] Curtailment or reinforcement risk identified
- [ ] Communications and monitoring requirements considered
- [ ] Construction power and temporary services identified

### G. Community and stakeholder context

- [ ] Farmer and landowner objectives documented
- [ ] Neighbouring land uses identified
- [ ] Local elected representatives and technical services identified
- [ ] Community concerns anticipated
- [ ] Existing agricultural, environmental, or community conflicts reviewed
- [ ] Public-access and recreational uses considered
- [ ] Local employment and supply-chain opportunities identified
- [ ] Stakeholder engagement sequence agreed

## 5.2.3 Stage-Gate Feasibility Process

A staged process prevents the project team from paying for detailed studies on sites that fail basic screening criteria.

### Stage 0: Desktop screening

**Typical duration:** 2–6 weeks

Review available maps, cadastral information, solar data, planning information, environmental designations, grid proximity, access, and agricultural records.

**Output:** A shortlisting matrix covering multiple candidate sites.

### Stage 1: Site visit and preliminary assessment

**Typical duration:** 1–2 months

Visit the site with the landowner or farmer. Confirm physical conditions, access, current agricultural practices, drainage, neighbouring uses, and visible environmental constraints.

**Output:** Site visit record, photographs, constraint map, and initial risk register.

### Stage 2: Preliminary technical and agricultural concept

**Typical duration:** 1–3 months

Develop one or more concept layouts. Test panel density, height, row spacing, equipment access, agricultural machinery, irrigation, drainage, and environmental buffers.

**Output:** Concept plan, preliminary capacity range, energy estimate, agricultural operating concept, and initial cost range.

### Stage 3: Detailed feasibility

**Typical duration:** 3–9 months or longer where seasonal surveys are needed

Complete the technical, agricultural, environmental, hydrological, grid, planning, stakeholder, and financial assessments required for an investment decision.

**Output:** Integrated feasibility report with a recommendation and conditions.

### Stage 4: Investment and development decision

The project sponsor should formally decide whether to:

- Proceed to development and permitting
- Redesign and repeat selected studies
- Reduce or change project scope
- Place the site on hold
- Reject the site

The decision should be recorded with assumptions, unresolved risks, required actions, and approval authority.

## 5.2.4 Technical Feasibility Assessment

### Solar resource and energy yield

The energy assessment should establish:

- Global horizontal and plane-of-array irradiance
- Seasonal and hourly production profile
- Temperature and wind assumptions
- Horizon and near-field shading losses
- Soiling, mismatch, availability, and degradation assumptions
- Inverter clipping and conversion losses
- Cable, transformer, and auxiliary-consumption losses
- Curtailment and grid export constraints
- Uncertainty range around annual energy production

Use conservative assumptions and document the source and period of each dataset. The model should distinguish between:

- **Resource uncertainty:** variation in solar conditions
- **Design uncertainty:** layout and equipment assumptions
- **Operational uncertainty:** availability, soiling, maintenance, and curtailment
- **Grid uncertainty:** connection date, export limit, and reinforcement requirements

A feasibility model should report a base case and lower-confidence cases rather than a single optimistic annual yield.

### Preliminary system sizing

Evaluate a range of configurations rather than selecting a single design too early:

| Configuration variable | Options to compare |
|---|---|
| Mounting | Fixed tilt, single-axis tracking, elevated or dynamic system |
| Module arrangement | Continuous rows, spaced rows, alternating or filtered coverage |
| Height | Low-clearance, machinery clearance, elevated agricultural clearance |
| Orientation | South-facing, east-west, bifacial-compatible layouts |
| Export strategy | Full export, limited export, self-consumption, storage-assisted |
| Foundation | Driven piles, screw piles, concrete, or other site-appropriate systems |
| Control | Fixed operation, seasonal adjustment, sensor-based control |

For each option, record energy output, land-take, agricultural compatibility, construction complexity, cost, maintenance access, and permitting implications.

### Layout and land-use efficiency

The preliminary layout should show:

- Parcel boundaries
- Existing roads and proposed access roads
- Panel rows and working corridors
- Inverters, transformers, substations, and cable routes
- Agricultural zones and exclusion areas
- Irrigation and drainage infrastructure
- Watercourses, wetlands, hedgerows, and habitat buffers
- Fire access and emergency routes
- Construction laydown and temporary facilities
- Areas reserved for biodiversity measures

The project should calculate both total project area and the effective agricultural area that remains accessible and productive.

### Structural and geotechnical screening

At feasibility stage, confirm the need for:

- Topographic survey
- Geotechnical investigation
- Pull-out or pile testing
- Flood and erosion assessment
- Wind and snow-load design
- Corrosion assessment
- Soil contamination screening where relevant
- Construction traffic and bearing-capacity review

Do not treat a generic mounting-system price as a site-specific structural solution. Ground conditions, slope, access, and agricultural machinery can materially change both design and cost.

## 5.2.5 Agricultural Feasibility Assessment

Agriculture is not a secondary land-use justification. It is one of the two core production systems and should have its own baseline, design criteria, budget, and performance indicators.

### Agricultural baseline

Document at least three years of available information where possible:

- Crops or livestock previously produced
- Yield and quality records
- Planting and harvest dates
- Input use, including water, fertilizer, and crop protection
- Machinery used and operating widths
- Labour requirements
- Sales channels and prices
- Weather-related losses
- Soil tests and amendment history
- Existing farm buildings and equipment

Where historical records are unavailable, use local reference farms and clearly label the assumptions.

### Crop and production-system compatibility

Assess how the PV design may affect:

- Photosynthetically active radiation
- Temperature and frost exposure
- Wind protection and turbulence
- Evapotranspiration and water demand
- Rainfall distribution beneath and between rows
- Soil moisture and drying patterns
- Disease and pest pressure
- Harvest timing and machinery access
- Product quality and marketability

Potential production systems include annual crops, horticulture, orchards, vines, forage, grazing, poultry, beekeeping, and combined systems. The correct choice depends on local climate, soils, farm skills, equipment, market access, and the final PV geometry.

### Agricultural design criteria

Before optimizing energy yield, define measurable agricultural requirements such as:

- Minimum machinery clearance
- Minimum row spacing and turning radius
- Maximum acceptable shade or coverage range
- Required uninterrupted production zones
- Irrigation access and pressure requirements
- Livestock shelter, fencing, and welfare requirements
- Harvest and emergency access
- Storage and loading requirements
- Seasonal access for maintenance and farming
- Minimum agricultural area and production target

### Agricultural performance baseline

The feasibility study should define how performance will be measured. Possible indicators include:

- Yield per hectare
- Product quality grade
- Gross agricultural revenue
- Water use per unit of output
- Input use per hectare
- Number of production cycles completed
- Livestock stocking rate and welfare indicators
- Soil organic matter and erosion indicators
- Percentage of land actively farmed

The project should not claim agricultural compatibility solely because farming is technically possible. It should define a realistic operating model and explain who will perform the work, with what equipment, and for which market.

## 5.2.6 Environmental and Biodiversity Baseline

### Baseline purpose

The environmental baseline provides the reference condition against which construction impacts, operational effects, and biodiversity improvements can be assessed. It also helps avoid placing infrastructure in the most sensitive areas.

### Baseline topics

Depending on site sensitivity and regulatory requirements, assess:

- Habitats and vegetation communities
- Hedgerows, woodland edges, wetlands, ponds, and watercourses
- Protected or notable species
- Birds and bats
- Pollinators and other invertebrates
- Mammals, reptiles, and amphibians
- Soil biological condition
- Surface water and groundwater sensitivity
- Landscape character and views
- Existing ecological management

Some ecological surveys are seasonal. The programme should allow enough time to collect representative data rather than treating a single site visit as a complete baseline.

### Environmental constraints map

Create a map that categorizes areas as:

1. **No-build areas:** legally protected, highly sensitive, unsafe, or technically unsuitable areas.
2. **Conditional areas:** areas that may be used only with specific design, mitigation, or monitoring measures.
3. **Preferred development areas:** lower-sensitivity areas with suitable access and infrastructure potential.
4. **Enhancement areas:** locations suitable for hedgerows, meadow strips, ponds, habitat corridors, or other measures.

### Biodiversity design principles

At feasibility stage:

- Retain valuable existing habitats where practicable
- Avoid fragmenting ecological corridors
- Maintain watercourse and wetland functions
- Limit unnecessary fencing and lighting
- Select vegetation and habitat measures suited to local conditions
- Design maintenance regimes that do not undermine ecological objectives
- Establish measurable biodiversity outcomes
- Include ecological measures in the budget and construction scope

Biodiversity measures should be designed as part of the project rather than added as decorative landscaping after the technical layout is complete.

## 5.2.7 Soil, Water, and Hydrology Assessment

### Soil assessment

A preliminary soil programme should consider:

- Texture and depth
- Structure and compaction
- pH and nutrient status
- Organic matter
- Drainage and water-holding capacity
- Erosion susceptibility
- Salinity or contamination where relevant
- Existing traffic damage
- Suitability for foundations and construction access

Take samples using a documented grid or sampling plan. Record sampling dates, depths, locations, laboratory methods, and interpretation criteria.

### Hydrology assessment

Map:

- Surface-water catchments
- Natural drainage lines
- Ditches, ponds, streams, and wetlands
- Flood-prone areas
- Groundwater protection sensitivities
- Existing irrigation and drainage infrastructure
- Likely changes to runoff during construction and operation

The feasibility design should explain how it will manage:

- Temporary construction runoff
- Sediment and erosion
- Concentrated discharge from panel edges
- Compaction and reduced infiltration
- Access-road drainage
- Extreme rainfall events
- Irrigation and water demand

### Water balance

Where irrigation is important, compare current and future water demand. Include:

- Crop water requirements
- Expected shading and evapotranspiration changes
- Irrigation efficiency
- Storage requirements
- Abstraction or supply constraints
- Backup arrangements during drought or equipment failure
- Water quality requirements

A reduction in water demand should be treated as a site-specific hypothesis to verify, not as an automatic project benefit.

## 5.2.8 Grid Connection Feasibility

Grid connection can become the critical path. Initiate contact with the relevant network operator early and document all assumptions.

### Preliminary grid questions

- Where is the nearest technically suitable connection point?
- What export capacity may be available?
- Is network reinforcement likely?
- What connection voltage and protection equipment may be required?
- What cable route and easements are needed?
- What is the expected connection queue or lead time?
- Are curtailment or export limitations possible?
- What telemetry, control, and communications systems are required?
- How will the connection cost be allocated?
- Does the project depend on storage or export limitation to remain viable?

### Grid deliverables

The feasibility file should contain, where available:

- Preliminary connection point
- Indicative export capacity
- Connection route map
- Budget estimate and cost assumptions
- Indicative programme
- Required studies and approvals
- Grid risks and mitigation actions
- Sensitivity case for delayed or reduced export

Do not present an indicative grid response as a binding connection offer. Track the status and validity date of each grid document.

## 5.2.9 Planning, Permitting, and Land-Use Screening

The feasibility team should identify the likely planning and environmental pathway before finalizing the concept. The detailed legal analysis belongs in Section 7, but the feasibility study should answer:

- Is the proposed land use compatible with applicable planning documents?
- Are there agricultural-land, landscape, heritage, or natural-area constraints?
- Is an environmental assessment or screening likely?
- Are there protected habitats, species, heritage assets, or landscape views that may affect the layout?
- What consultation or public-participation steps may apply?
- Are there restrictions on access roads, fences, buildings, or grid infrastructure?
- What evidence will be needed to demonstrate continued agricultural use?
- Which authority or authorities should be contacted before submitting an application?

### Permitting risk classification

| Risk level | Typical condition | Feasibility response |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Clear land-use pathway and limited sensitivity | Proceed with standard studies and pre-consultation |
| Medium | Several constraints or uncertain interpretation | Obtain specialist advice and authority feedback |
| High | Protected area, major landscape sensitivity, disputed agricultural use, or complex grid works | Do not commit to final design until constraints are resolved |

The result should be a permitting route map showing approvals, responsible parties, dependencies, expected decision points, and evidence requirements.

## 5.2.10 Stakeholder and Community Assessment

Stakeholder engagement should begin before the design becomes fixed. Early discussions can identify practical constraints that are not visible in desktop data.

### Stakeholder groups

- Landowner and farmer/operator
- Agricultural tenants or neighbouring farmers
- Local elected officials and municipal technical staff
- Planning and environmental authorities
- Grid operator
- Water authorities and users
- Residents and neighbouring properties
- Agricultural associations and chambers
- Environmental organizations
- Emergency services
- Financing partners and insurers
- Future O&M contractors

### Stakeholder assessment questions

- Who can approve, delay, influence, or prevent the project?
- What interests or concerns does each stakeholder have?
- What information do they need?
- When should they be consulted?
- Who owns the response to each issue?
- Which commitments must be documented?

### Stakeholder register

| Stakeholder | Interest or authority | Potential concern | Engagement action | Owner | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Farmer/operator | Agricultural viability | Machinery access and yield | Co-design workshops | Project manager | Open |
| Municipality | Local planning and community | Landscape and traffic | Pre-application meeting | Development lead | Open |
| Grid operator | Connection approval | Capacity and protection | Technical enquiry | Electrical lead | Open |
| Neighbours | Local amenity | Views, noise, drainage | Information meeting | Community lead | Open |
| Environmental advisor | Ecological evidence | Habitats and species | Baseline survey | Environmental lead | Open |

Record commitments, questions, objections, and responses. Avoid promising outcomes that depend on later approvals or studies.

## 5.2.11 Financial Feasibility and Scenario Testing

The financial model should use outputs from the technical, agricultural, grid, permitting, and environmental assessments. It should not be built from generic cost assumptions alone.

### Core financial inputs

- Installed DC and AC capacity
- Annual energy yield and degradation
- Export limit and curtailment assumption
- Electricity price or offtake arrangement
- Agricultural production and revenue
- Land lease or revenue-sharing structure
- Grid connection cost
- Development and consulting costs
- PV, mounting, civil, electrical, irrigation, and agricultural infrastructure costs
- Financing structure and interest rate assumptions
- Insurance, taxes, O&M, labour, and monitoring costs
- Replacement and decommissioning provisions
- Contingency reserve

### Minimum scenarios

1. **Base case:** Most defensible current assumptions.
2. **Low-energy case:** Lower solar resource, higher losses, or greater curtailment.
3. **Low-agriculture case:** Reduced yield, delayed establishment, or higher operating cost.
4. **High-cost case:** Grid reinforcement, construction inflation, or difficult ground conditions.
5. **Delay case:** Permitting, financing, or connection delay.
6. **Combined downside case:** Multiple adverse assumptions occurring together.

The output should show the effect on total investment, annual cash flow, debt service, payback, NPV, IRR, and required equity. A project that works only under optimistic assumptions should not be classified as financially robust.

## 5.2.12 Feasibility Report Structure

A recommended feasibility report structure is:

1. Executive summary
2. Project objectives and scope
3. Site description and land control
4. Existing agricultural system
5. Solar resource and energy assessment
6. Agrivoltaic design options
7. Agricultural feasibility and operating model
8. Soil, water, and hydrology assessment
9. Environmental and biodiversity baseline
10. Grid connection assessment
11. Planning and permitting pathway
12. Stakeholder engagement summary
13. Construction and logistics assessment
14. Preliminary O&M concept
15. Capital and operating cost estimate
16. Financial model and scenario analysis
17. Risk register and mitigation plan
18. Recommended option and decision conditions
19. Study limitations and information gaps
20. Appendices: maps, surveys, calculations, correspondence, and assumptions

### Quality-control requirements

Before issuing the report:

- Confirm that all maps use the same coordinate reference system
- Cross-check parcel areas against cadastral and survey information
- Reconcile the energy model with the layout and equipment list
- Reconcile agricultural area with access, exclusion, and infrastructure areas
- Check that environmental constraints are reflected in the concept plan
- Check that grid assumptions are clearly labelled as indicative or confirmed
- Review all costs for scope omissions and double counting
- Record the date and source of each important assumption
- Obtain review comments from the farmer/operator and technical leads
- Identify information that must be updated before financial close

## 5.2.13 Go/No-Go Decision Framework

The decision should be based on thresholds agreed before the result is known. Example criteria are shown below and should be adapted to the project.

| Decision area | Proceed when | Rework or stop when |
|---|---|---|
| Land control | Access and tenure pathway is credible | Ownership, tenancy, or access remains unresolved |
| Agriculture | A funded, workable production model exists | Agriculture is only nominal or operationally impractical |
| Solar | Yield and uncertainty are acceptable | Output depends on unsupported assumptions |
| Environment | Impacts can be avoided or mitigated | Sensitive areas cannot be protected |
| Water and soil | Drainage, erosion, and water supply are manageable | Material risks remain unmitigated |
| Grid | Capacity, cost, and programme are acceptable | Connection makes the project uneconomic |
| Permitting | A credible pathway and evidence plan exist | Major approval risk has not been investigated |
| Stakeholders | Key parties are engaged and issues are tractable | Fundamental objections remain unresolved |
| Finance | Downside cases remain investable | Viability depends on the base case alone |
| Delivery | Team, budget, and schedule are credible | Critical skills or resources are unavailable |

### Decision categories

- **Go:** Proceed to development, permitting, and detailed design.
- **Go with conditions:** Proceed only after defined actions are completed.
- **Rework:** Revise layout, agricultural model, or financing and reassess.
- **Hold:** Preserve the option while waiting for external information.
- **No-go:** Stop development and document the reasons.

Every decision should identify an accountable approver, date, conditions, and next review point.

## 5.2.14 Common Feasibility Risks and Mitigation

| Risk | Consequence | Early indicator | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agricultural model is not credible | Planning, financing, or operational failure | No named operator or yield baseline | Co-design with farmer and independent agricultural review |
| Grid capacity is overstated | Delay, redesign, or uneconomic connection | Only desktop distance considered | Obtain early network-operator feedback and model export limits |
| Seasonal ecology is missed | Permit delay or redesign | Single out-of-season site visit | Plan surveys across relevant seasons |
| Ground conditions are poor | Higher foundations and civil costs | No geotechnical evidence | Commission appropriate ground investigation before final layout |
| Water supply is unreliable | Crop failure or operating conflict | Informal irrigation assumption | Verify rights, supply, storage, and drought plan |
| Access is inadequate | Construction delay and agricultural disruption | Narrow roads or weak bridges | Complete logistics and route survey early |
| Community concerns emerge late | Objections and schedule delay | No stakeholder register | Start transparent engagement before design freeze |
| Costs are understated | Funding gap or reduced returns | Generic cost per MW used | Obtain supplier and contractor budget quotes |
| Benefits are overstated | Weak decision and reputational risk | No baseline or monitoring plan | Define measurable baselines and uncertainty ranges |

## 5.2.15 Recommended Feasibility Work Programme

A typical work programme for a medium-sized project may be organized as follows:

| Month | Activity | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | Desktop screening and land review | Candidate-site matrix |
| 1–2 | Site visits and stakeholder introductions | Site record and issues log |
| 2–4 | Concept layouts and preliminary energy model | Options appraisal |
| 3–6 | Agricultural, soil, water, and environmental baseline work | Baseline reports |
| 4–7 | Grid and permitting pre-consultation | Connection and permitting route |
| 5–8 | Cost, revenue, and financing model | Financial scenarios |
| 7–9 | Integrated review and design refinement | Preferred concept |
| 8–10 | Feasibility report and independent review | Investment recommendation |
| 10–12 | Decision gate and development authorization | Go/no-go decision |

The programme should be adjusted where surveys are seasonal, grid studies have long lead times, or land and stakeholder negotiations are complex.

## Conclusion

A feasibility study should convert a promising site into an evidence-based development decision. The most important outcome is not a polished concept drawing; it is a transparent understanding of whether the project can simultaneously deliver agricultural value, energy production, environmental responsibility, grid access, regulatory compliance, and financial resilience.

The strongest studies are interdisciplinary. They involve the farmer, solar and electrical engineers, environmental specialists, soil and water experts, planners, grid representatives, financiers, and local stakeholders early enough for their findings to influence the design. They also make uncertainty visible and reserve detailed commitments for the issues that have been tested.

Once the feasibility stage is complete, the project should move into detailed financing, approvals, design, and procurement only if the decision conditions are clear, owned, and realistically achievable.
