# Section 6.2: Soil Health and Hydrology

## Introduction

Agrivoltaic projects can change soil conditions and water movement through shading, rainfall redistribution from panel edges, foundations, access routes, construction traffic, and vegetation management. Outcomes depend on the soil, slope, climate, array design, agricultural use, and construction practice. Shading may reduce soil-surface heating and evaporation in some settings, but it does not guarantee improved soil moisture or crop water availability. Likewise, a project may maintain infiltration where soil structure and cover are protected, while compaction and bare ground can increase runoff and erosion.

Define soil and water objectives early, use a documented baseline, and adapt the design and management to site conditions. The [open-access agrivoltaics study by Barron-Gafford et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211631/) reports crop-, climate-, and location-dependent water-use outcomes; it is evidence of potential, not a universal prediction for French sites. For soil-health policy and assessment context, see the [European Commission's soil health resources](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/topics/soil-and-land/soil-health_en) and France's [GIS Sol soil information resources](https://www.gissol.fr/).

This section is a practical planning guide, not legal advice. Project-specific requirements should be confirmed with qualified French agricultural, soil, hydrology, and planning professionals and the competent authorities.

## 6.2.1 How Agrivoltaics Affect Soil and Water

### Soil moisture and evapotranspiration

Panels alter the amount and distribution of sunlight, wind, and rainfall reaching the ground. Shaded areas may have lower soil-surface temperatures and evaporation during some periods, while panel edges can receive concentrated rainfall and other areas may receive less direct precipitation. The net effect on soil moisture, evapotranspiration, crop water use, and yield varies with panel height and spacing, season, crop, soil texture and depth, and local weather. Measure or model those effects for the proposed layout rather than treating shade as a substitute for irrigation or as a guaranteed water-saving measure.

The [agrivoltaics field study by Barron-Gafford et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211631/) describes potential water-use benefits in a dryland setting. For French site context, use soil data from [GIS Sol](https://www.gissol.fr/) alongside local climate, crop, and water-resource information.

### Infiltration, runoff, and erosion

Soil infiltration depends on soil structure, surface cover, slope, antecedent moisture, and land management. Compacted tracks, exposed soil, concentrated panel-edge runoff, and poorly placed outlets can increase overland flow, rilling, sediment movement, or local ponding. Continuous vegetation and intact soil structure can help protect the surface, but do not eliminate the need to check flow paths and drainage capacity.

Assess erosion susceptibility and runoff pathways during site selection and design. The European Commission's [Joint Research Centre soil erosion resources](https://esdac.jrc.ec.europa.eu/themes/soil-erosion) describe erosion assessment at European scale; site-level field checks remain necessary. For French water and catchment information, consult [Eaufrance](https://www.eaufrance.fr/) and the relevant basin authority.

### Compaction and soil structure

Construction and maintenance traffic can compress soil, reduce pore space, restrict root growth, and impair infiltration. Risk is higher when soil is wet, when heavy machinery repeatedly crosses the same ground, or when access is not confined to planned routes. Compaction can persist after construction and should be identified and corrected before it compromises agricultural use.

Soil structure and its capacity to provide functions such as water storage and filtration are central to the [EU soil-health framework](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/topics/soil-and-land/soil-health_en). The [American Farmland Trust Solar Soil Health Guide](https://farmlandinfo.org/publications/solar-soil-health-guide/) provides practical recommendations for protecting agricultural soils during solar development.

## 6.2.2 Soil Health Objectives

Before construction, define a short set of measurable objectives suited to the site's soil, farm system, and hydrology. Depending on the baseline and project risks, objectives may include:

- Maintain soil structure and rooting conditions in productive areas.
- Avoid persistent compaction in crop, forage, or grazing zones.
- Maintain or restore ground cover to limit erosion and sediment movement.
- Protect topsoil and retain its productive function where earthworks are necessary.
- Preserve infiltration and avoid creating concentrated or damaging runoff.
- Prevent sediment-laden water from leaving the site or entering sensitive areas.
- Maintain drainage and avoid harmful ponding, waterlogging, or drying of neighboring land.

Distinguish construction-phase protection from operational soil management and water management. Record the baseline, target, method, location, review date, and responsible party for each objective. Objectives should reflect local conditions; do not set a target to improve a soil indicator before confirming that it can be measured consistently.

## 6.2.3 Design and Management Measures

### Use soil and water information before fixing the layout

Review available soil mapping, topography, drainage, watercourses, flood information, and agricultural history before fixing panel rows, foundations, tracks, and cable routes. [GIS Sol](https://www.gissol.fr/) provides French soil information, while [BRGM's InfoTerre portal](https://infoterre.brgm.fr/) provides geological and subsurface information. These datasets support screening and should be supplemented by site inspection and, where needed, field investigation.

Identify steep or erosion-prone ground, wet or poorly drained areas, shallow or vulnerable soils, existing drainage features, and locations where water concentrates. Avoid sensitive areas where practicable; otherwise adapt the layout, construction method, or protection measures to the documented risk.

### Protect topsoil and soil structure

Minimize earthworks and the area disturbed. Where stripping is necessary, define limits and handling methods in the works plan. Keep topsoil separate from subsoil, avoid mixing or trafficking stored topsoil, and locate stockpiles away from drainage routes. Protect stockpiles against erosion and reinstate soil horizons appropriately during restoration. The [AFT Solar Soil Health Guide](https://farmlandinfo.org/publications/solar-soil-health-guide/) gives practical guidance on soil protection and restoration for solar projects.

### Manage runoff and drainage

Design the array, access routes, and drainage so that rainfall is not discharged as concentrated flow onto vulnerable soil, neighboring land, or water features. Check panel-edge drip lines and outlet locations as well as the overall catchment. Depending on site conditions, suitable measures may include:

- Retaining existing vegetation and drainage features where appropriate.
- Maintaining vegetated inter-rows, buffers, or contour-aligned strips.
- Dispersing flow and using stable, vegetated conveyance routes.
- Providing appropriately sized infiltration or attenuation features where soils and groundwater conditions permit.
- Stabilizing outlets and adding sediment controls during exposed-soil works.
- Keeping access tracks and hard surfaces to the minimum needed.

Use local soil and groundwater information to check whether infiltration features are suitable; infiltration is not appropriate at every site. Inspect drainage after significant rainfall and maintain it without creating faster or more erosive discharge.

### Maintain vegetation cover and coordinate farm operations

Ground cover protects soil from raindrop impact, slows surface flow, and supports soil biological activity. Plan a vegetation regime compatible with the agricultural production, expected shade, machinery, grazing, and fire-safety requirements. Limit bare ground to areas and periods where it is necessary, and re-establish cover promptly after works or damage.

Coordinate crop rotations, grazing pressure, harvest, irrigation, and maintenance access with the farmer. Avoid heavy traffic on vulnerable or saturated soil, and use designated routes to limit repeated disturbance.

## 6.2.4 Construction Controls

Construction is often the period of greatest risk to soil structure and drainage. Before mobilization, translate the soil and water baseline into clear contractor requirements.

### Before works

- Map sensitive soils, drainage features, runoff pathways, and exclusion areas.
- Identify soil stripping, storage, reinstatement, and restoration requirements.
- Define designated traffic routes, equipment laydown areas, and stockpile locations.
- Set wet-weather restrictions and the person responsible for stopping work.
- Install erosion and sediment controls before disturbing soil where needed.
- Brief contractors on pollution prevention, soil handling, and incident reporting.

### During works

- Keep construction and delivery traffic on designated routes.
- Avoid working or driving on saturated soil where practicable.
- Separate topsoil and subsoil where excavation requires removal.
- Keep exposed areas small and stabilize them as soon as practicable.
- Maintain temporary drainage and sediment controls; inspect them regularly and after rainfall.
- Prevent concrete washout, fuels, and other pollutants from entering soil, drains, or water.
- Record damage, corrective action, and any departure from the agreed plan.

### Restoration

Restore disturbed ground promptly using the agreed soil profile and suitable ground cover. Inspect for rutting, compaction, erosion, ponding, unstable outlets, and sediment beyond the work area. Correct defects and confirm that agricultural access and drainage function as intended before closing out construction controls.

## 6.2.5 Baseline, Monitoring, and Adaptive Management

### Establish a baseline

Before construction, document information proportionate to the site's size and risk. This may include:

- Soil type, depth, condition, and any available soil-map information.
- Slope, drainage features, watercourses, and runoff pathways.
- Existing compaction, erosion, ponding, and land-management history.
- Ground cover and representative photographs at fixed locations.
- Infiltration or soil-structure observations where appropriate.
- Existing agricultural performance and known water constraints.

Record methods, locations, dates, weather, and limitations so that later observations can be compared fairly. For regional screening, use [GIS Sol](https://www.gissol.fr/), [BRGM InfoTerre](https://infoterre.brgm.fr/), [Eaufrance](https://www.eaufrance.fr/), and the [EU Joint Research Centre's soil data and erosion resources](https://esdac.jrc.ec.europa.eu/).

### Monitor during construction and operation

Select a small number of practical indicators linked to the objectives. These may include:

- Visible erosion, rilling, sediment deposits, or exposed soil.
- Ground-cover condition and bare-ground extent.
- Rutting or compaction along access routes and in productive areas.
- Ponding, drainage obstruction, or new concentrated runoff.
- Soil moisture in representative shaded, unshaded, and panel-edge locations where relevant.
- Crop or forage condition and any changes to irrigation needs.
- Incidents, inspections, and completed corrective actions.

Inspect during construction, after restoration, and after significant rainfall. In early operation, review the indicators seasonally and after relevant maintenance or agricultural changes. Use consistent methods and compare like locations and seasons.

### Review and adapt

Review results with the farmer, project owner, and relevant technical advisers. If indicators show degradation, identify the likely cause and adjust traffic routes, wet-weather restrictions, vegetation management, drainage, or sediment controls. Repair compacted or eroded areas and verify the result. Keep a record of findings, decisions, responsible parties, and follow-up dates.

The EU's [soil-health policy and monitoring information](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/topics/soil-and-land/soil-health_en) provides broader context for monitoring soil condition. Project indicators and frequency should remain proportionate to local risks and any applicable approval conditions.

## 6.2.6 French Project and Planning Context

Integrate soil and hydrology into site selection, agricultural design, environmental assessment, and construction planning. In France, confirm current and site-specific requirements with the competent authorities and qualified professionals, including whether proposed works trigger water-related procedures or constraints. The [French Environmental Code](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/id/LEGITEXT000006074220/) and [Energy Code](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/id/LEGITEXT000023983208/) are available on Légifrance; applicable provisions depend on the project, works, and location. Consult [Eaufrance](https://www.eaufrance.fr/) and the relevant DDT(M), water agency, or basin authority for local water-management context.

Soil and hydrology assessment should support the demonstration that the proposed agrivoltaic design remains compatible with agricultural use and site constraints. Check applicable planning and environmental assessment requirements early, and document how soil protection, drainage, and restoration will be implemented. This guide does not determine whether a particular project requires an authorization or water procedure.

---

## 6.2.7 Project Checklist

- [ ] Map soil, slope, drainage, water features, and runoff pathways before fixing the design.
- [ ] Define site-specific, measurable soil and water objectives against a documented baseline.
- [ ] Minimize earthworks and protect topsoil and soil structure where disturbance is unavoidable.
- [ ] Keep construction traffic on designated routes and define wet-weather restrictions.
- [ ] Maintain vegetation cover and limit bare ground.
- [ ] Check panel-edge runoff, drainage outlets, and receiving areas for erosion risk.
- [ ] Install and maintain erosion and sediment controls during construction.
- [ ] Restore disturbed areas promptly and inspect after significant rainfall.
- [ ] Monitor compaction, ground cover, runoff, and agricultural condition; adapt management when needed.
- [ ] Confirm current French planning, environmental, and water-related requirements with competent authorities.
